*Evidence dossier compiled 2026-05-11.  Comparator countries: Qatar, UAE, Saudi Arabia, Oman, Algeria, Iraq, with Norway and Australia as fiscal-regime bookends.*

## 1. Qatar — the prime comparator

### Ownership and corporate structure

QatarEnergy (the rebranded Qatar Petroleum, renamed October 2021) is 100% owned by the State of Qatar and is the sole operator of all upstream hydrocarbon activity in Qatar's territory.  QatarEnergy LNG (formerly Qatargas) holds the four mega-trains at Ras Laffan and is majority-owned by QatarEnergy with the remaining minority interests held by international oil company (IOC) partners — primarily ExxonMobil, Shell, TotalEnergies, ConocoPhillips, Eni, and Sinopec — through joint-venture vehicles.[^1]

### Fiscal terms applied to IOC partners

Qatar's Income Tax Law (Law No. 24 of 2018) sets a flat headline corporate income tax rate of 10% for foreign companies, but the law explicitly carves out petroleum operations.  Under Article 11 (read alongside Law No. 3 of 2007 on oil and gas), the rate **applied to oil and gas operations may not be less than 35%**, and where pre-2010 government agreements do not specify a rate, the default rate is 35%.[^2]

That 35% rate is layered on top of the upstream royalty.  Qatar does not publish a single statutory royalty rate for LNG in a public-domain document, but in published Joint Venture agreements (e.g. the QatarEnergy / ExxonMobil RasGas DSAs disclosed in U.S. SEC filings during the original RasGas bond issuances of 2005–2014) the **royalty on natural gas production has historically run at 10% of the wellhead value**, with separate volume/value royalties payable on associated condensate and LNG.[^3]  This 10% / 35% stack — combined with the IOCs taking only a minority share of profit (state typically retaining 60–70% of profit in JV terms) — produces an effective government take per LNG cargo materially higher than under Australia's PRRT regime.

`UNVERIFIED to primary licence text`: the precise royalty schedule for the post-2010 North Field expansion projects (NFE, NFS, NFW) is not in the public domain; QatarEnergy treats the underlying DPSAs as confidential.  The 10% figure is the most-cited number in industry-tracker literature (Wood Mackenzie summaries; Energy Intelligence reporting) and is consistent with the post-2014 international service-and-royalty norm in the Gulf.

### IMF Article IV — the headline fiscal evidence

The IMF concluded its 2024 Article IV Consultation with Qatar on 31 January 2025; the staff report was published as **IMF Country Report No. 25/47** on 24 February 2025.[^4]  Headline findings:

* Qatar's overall fiscal balance recorded a surplus of **5.5% of GDP in 2023**, narrowed from the 2022 surplus, on the back of softer hydrocarbon prices.[^4]
* The IMF projected fiscal and current-account surpluses would remain through the medium term but decline as a share of GDP before LNG expansion volumes kick in.[^4]
* The Fund flagged that "Qatar's LNG production expansion is set to generate significant hydrocarbon revenues and maintain the twin surpluses, assuming continued fiscal discipline."[^4]

### Qatar 2024 fiscal outturn — the official budget figures

Qatar's Ministry of Finance reported the 2024 fiscal outturn as the kingdom's **fourth consecutive budget surplus**, despite lower prices.[^5]

* Total revenue: **USD 58.6 bn** (down 16% on 2023's ~USD 69.6 bn)
* **Hydrocarbon revenue: USD 47.5 bn** (down ~USD 10 bn on 2023's ~USD 57.5 bn)
* Non-hydrocarbon revenue: USD 11.1 bn
* Fiscal surplus: USD 1.6 bn (with hydrocarbons making up **81% of all government revenue**)[^5]

The 2023 USD 57.5 bn hydrocarbon-revenue figure (~A$87 bn at 2023 average exchange rates) corroborates the Australia Institute's headline that the Qatari government extracted **roughly A$56 bn from LNG alone in 2023** — the LNG slice of total hydrocarbon receipts (hydrocarbons include condensate, NGLs, and some oil) is approximately two-thirds.[^6]

### LNG production capacity

Qatar's nameplate LNG capacity stood at **~77 Mtpa at end-2023**.[^7]  In February 2024, Energy Minister Saad Sherida Al-Kaabi announced the addition of the **North Field West (NFW) project**, lifting the planned 2030 target to **142 Mtpa**, an ~85% increase on 2023 capacity.[^7]  The intermediate capacity steps are:
* North Field East (NFE): adds ~32 Mtpa, online 2025–2026 → ~110 Mtpa
* North Field South (NFS): adds ~16 Mtpa, online ~2027 → ~126 Mtpa
* North Field West (NFW): adds ~16 Mtpa, online ~2030 → ~142 Mtpa

Capital expenditure across the three NFE/NFS/NFW phases is in the USD 50–60 bn range.

### Qatar Investment Authority — sovereign wealth accumulation

The Qatar Investment Authority (QIA), founded 2005 to channel hydrocarbon surpluses into international assets, held **~USD 557 bn AUM as of August 2025** per the most recent triangulated estimate (QIA does not publish quarterly AUM).[^8]  The Sovereign Wealth Fund Institute ranking places QIA #9 globally.  At a Qatari national-citizen population (excluding non-citizen residents) of approximately 380,000, QIA AUM is roughly **USD 1.47 million per Qatari citizen**, or — at the broader resident-population denominator of ~3.1 million — approximately **USD 180,000 per resident**.[^9]

### Per-capita gas revenue (the comparator number Australians will feel)

* Qatar 2024 hydrocarbon government revenue: USD 47.5 bn
* Resident population (2025): 3.12 m[^9]
* **Per-resident hydrocarbon revenue: USD 15,224 / year**
* Per-citizen hydrocarbon revenue (citizens only): **~USD 125,000 / year**

For comparison, Australia's per-capita PRRT take in 2024-25 (AUD 1.347 bn / 26.7 m residents) is approximately **AUD 50 per person**, or **~USD 33**.  Even including the company-tax component of LNG-related corporate tax (which is not isolatable but is materially smaller than Qatar's per-tonne take), Australia's per-capita LNG revenue is two orders of magnitude below Qatar's.

## 2. United Arab Emirates — gas + diversification

### Ownership and the ADNOC corporate restructure

ADNOC (Abu Dhabi National Oil Company) is 100% state-owned by the Emirate of Abu Dhabi.  Since 2017, ADNOC has executed a phased partial-monetisation strategy: it has IPO'd or sold minority stakes in:
* **ADNOC Distribution** (the fuel-retail arm; IPO 2017, 13% free float)
* **ADNOC Drilling** (IPO 2021, 11% free float)
* **ADNOC Gas** (IPO Feb 2023, 5% free float)
* **Borouge** (petrochemicals JV with Borealis; IPO 2022)
* **ADNOC L&S** (logistics; IPO 2023)

This is a deliberate model: monetise non-strategic subsidiaries on the Abu Dhabi exchange (ADX) and recycle proceeds into Mubadala / ADIA, while keeping the upstream concessions 100% state-controlled.

### Domestic vs export gas balance

The UAE is **not** historically a major LNG exporter — it is in fact a net LNG importer to its northern emirates via the Dolphin pipeline and Jebel Ali regas terminal.  This is the key contrast with Qatar: while the UAE sits on world-class associated and non-associated gas reserves, much is reinjected for enhanced oil recovery (EOR) or consumed domestically for power, desalination, and feedstock.  The UAE is a major **destination market** for Australian LNG, not a major competing exporter.

### Ruwais LNG — the UAE's first export-scale LNG buildout

In June 2024 ADNOC took Final Investment Decision (FID) on the **Ruwais LNG project**: 2 × 4.8 Mtpa trains, **9.6 Mtpa total capacity**, EPC contract worth USD 5.5 bn awarded to Technip Energies + JGC + NMDC.[^10]  First train online 2028, second 2029.  Material for Australia: this is the first credible new ME LNG capacity outside Qatar, and Shell has signed a 15-year SPA for >8 Mtpa of offtake.

### ADNOC Gas — the IPO-disclosed financials

ADNOC Gas FY2024 disclosed (LSE filings):
* Adjusted revenue: **USD 24.43 bn** (+7% YoY)
* Net income: **USD 5.0 bn**
* EBITDA: USD 8.65 bn (35% margin)
* Total dividend declared FY2024: **USD 3.412 bn**[^11]

Because the UAE government (via ADNOC) holds ~95% of ADNOC Gas equity, ~USD 3.24 bn of that dividend flowed back to the state.  This is in addition to royalties, corporate income tax under the new 9% UAE federal CIT (introduced June 2023), and the upstream economic-interest fees ADNOC charges its various concession holders.

### Sovereign wealth funds — UAE has multiple

| Fund | AUM (USD) | As of | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Abu Dhabi Investment Authority (ADIA) | ~1,110 bn (estimate) | 2025 | [^12] |
| Mubadala Investment Company | 385 bn | end-2025 | [^13] |
| ADQ | ~250 bn (estimate) | 2025 | [^12] |

Aggregate UAE sovereign wealth AUM is now in the order of **USD 1.7–1.8 trillion**, putting the UAE per-capita SWF figure (population ~10 m) at around **USD 175,000 per resident** — comparable to Norway on a per-resident basis.

### UAE budget — hydrocarbon share

The IMF's most recent UAE Article IV (concluded July 2024) noted that hydrocarbon revenues fund roughly **35–40% of the consolidated federal budget**, with the balance from corporate income tax (UAE introduced a 9% federal CIT on profits >AED 375k in June 2023) and federal taxes/fees.  This is materially lower than Qatar's 81% — UAE has aggressively diversified its non-hydrocarbon revenue base since 2018 VAT introduction.

## 3. Saudi Arabia — gas in a primarily oil regime

### Saudi Aramco IPO-era disclosures

Saudi Aramco's IPO (December 2019, 1.5% free float on Tadawul) forced the company to publish detailed prospectus-level fiscal disclosures.  The current government-take stack on Aramco's upstream barrels:

**Royalty on crude oil and condensate** (effective 1 January 2020):[^14]
| Brent price band | Marginal royalty rate |
|---|---|
| Up to USD 70/bbl | 15% |
| USD 70–100/bbl | 45% (marginal) |
| Above USD 100/bbl | 80% (marginal) |

**Royalty on natural gas (methane and ethane):** USD 0.00 / mmBTU
**Royalty on NGLs (propane, butane, natural gasoline):** USD 0.035 / mmBTU[^14]

The zero gas royalty is critical context: Saudi gas (the Master Gas System; Jafurah unconventional development) is **almost entirely directed at domestic power, water, and petrochemical feedstock**, not export.  Saudi government extracts value from gas through the downstream value chain (petrochemicals, refined products) and through Aramco's overall dividend stream, not through gas-specific royalties.

**Income tax on Aramco:** 50% on upstream business, 20% on downstream.[^14]

**Dividend mechanism:** In FY2024 Aramco paid **USD 124 bn in total dividends** (base + performance-linked).[^15]  The Saudi state (direct + PIF holdings) owns 81.5% of Aramco; that translates to approximately **USD 101 bn flowing to the government as dividend in 2024 alone**, on top of royalties and corporate tax.

Aramco's published net income for FY2024 was **USD 106.2 bn (SAR 398.4 bn)**, down 12.4% from FY2023.[^15]

### Saudi PIF — accumulation vehicle

The Public Investment Fund reported AUM of **USD 913 bn at end-2024**, up 19% YoY.[^16]  Vision 2030 target is **USD 2 trillion by 2030**.  Unlike QIA or ADIA, PIF is funded only partly from petroleum receipts; it also runs concentrated equity bets (Lucid, Newcastle United, LIV Golf) and receives direct asset transfers from the Ministry of Finance (Aramco shares transferred in 2022 and 2023).

### Per-capita Saudi metrics

Population (2024): ~33 m residents (of which ~21 m citizens).
Government revenue per resident from Aramco dividend alone (2024): **~USD 3,060 per resident**, or **~USD 4,800 per citizen**.  This is materially below Qatar's per-capita figure but vastly above Australia's.

## 4. Oman — LNG exporter, the closest small-LNG comparator

### Oman LNG ownership

Oman LNG (operator of three trains at Qalhat, Sur):
* Government of Oman: **51%**
* Shell: 30%
* TotalEnergies: 5.54%
* Korea LNG: 5%
* Mitsubishi: 2.77%
* Mitsui: 2.77%
* PTTEP: 2%
* Itochu: 0.9%[^17]

### Production

Nameplate capacity 10.4 Mtpa across three trains.  Actual 2024 output: **11.98 Mtpa** (debottlenecking has lifted utilisation above nameplate).[^17]  Marsa LNG (TotalEnergies-led; FID 2024; ~1 Mtpa) and a planned fourth Qalhat train are in the pipeline but materially smaller than the Qatari or Australian build-out.

### Fiscal performance

The IMF's 2024 Article IV consultation with Oman (Country Report 25/13, January 2025) reported:[^18]
* **Overall fiscal balance: +6.2% of GDP in 2024**
* Current account balance: +2.4% of GDP
* Government debt: 35% of GDP (down from a 2020 peak above 60%)
* Government revenue heavily concentrated in hydrocarbons, though Oman has been the most aggressive Gulf state on VAT (introduced 2021) and personal income tax (announced 2024, taking effect 2028 — the first PIT in the GCC).

Oman has **no sovereign wealth fund of QIA scale**; the Oman Investment Authority (OIA) consolidated several state funds in 2020 and reports AUM around **USD 50 bn**.  Oman's fiscal strategy has been debt reduction first, SWF accumulation second.

## 5. Algeria — pipeline-plus-LNG and the 2019 reset

### Sonatrach

Sonatrach is 100% Algerian-state-owned and is the sole upstream operator with the right to enter into joint ventures with IOCs.

### The 2019 hydrocarbon law (Law 19-13)

After a decade of stagnation in upstream investment, Algeria's 2019 Law 19-13 substantially **softened** the fiscal regime to attract IOC capital.  Headline changes:[^19]
* Government share of a typical gas-field economics **dropped from 80% to 56–69%** depending on contract type
* IOC internal rate of return expected to rise from 5–10% to 19–23%
* Three new contract templates: Participation Agreement (Sonatrach ≥51%), Production Sharing Agreement (PSA), Risk Service Agreement (RSA)
* Customs duties and VAT exemptions on imported E&P equipment

The 2024 licensing round (the first in a decade) was launched under this regime.

### LNG exports

Algeria has two operational LNG terminals (Arzew GL1Z/GL2Z/GL3Z and Skikda GL1K/GL2K), all 100% Sonatrach-owned and operated, with a combined nameplate capacity of ~25 Mtpa but actual utilisation well below.
* 2023 LNG exports: ~17 bcm (~12.6 Mt)
* 2024 LNG exports: 14.5 bcm (~10.7 Mt), -14% YoY[^19]

Most of Algeria's gas to Europe travels by pipeline (Medgaz, Transmed) rather than as LNG.

### Fiscal position

Per the IMF's 2025 Article IV consultation (Country Report 25/271, September 2025):[^20]
* **Hydrocarbon revenues = 67% of total revenue (period average)**
* **Fiscal deficit widened to -13.9% of GDP in 2024** as expenditure surged and prices softened
* IMF flags "high" sovereign-stress risk absent ~5% of GDP fiscal consolidation 2025–28
* No major SWF.  Algeria runs a Revenue Regulation Fund (FRR) which has been heavily drawn down.

Algeria is the cautionary tale: high state ownership and high government take are **necessary but not sufficient** for fiscal resilience — Algeria's expenditure mix (subsidies, wages) has eroded the buffer that Qatar and Norway preserved.

## 6. Iraq — the sanity comparator

### Hydrocarbon revenue management

Iraq is structurally over-reliant on oil receipts.  In 2024:
* Oil exports = **91% of federal revenue** (~USD 107 bn total federal revenue)[^21]
* Oil production: 3.86 mbpd (-6.2% on 2023)[^22]

### Contract structure — NOT PSCs

Counter to the common framing, Iraq's federal contracts with IOCs are predominantly **Technical Service Contracts (TSCs)**, not Production Sharing Contracts.  Under the TSC model:
* BP / CNPC (Basra Energy Co JV): operates Rumaila
* ExxonMobil + Shell originally on West Qurna Phase 1; **ExxonMobil exited in January 2024**, handing operatorship to PetroChina
* Eni: Zubair
* TotalEnergies: Gas Growth Integrated Project (GGIP) signed 2023
* CNOOC: Missan group

The state retains all produced barrels and pays IOCs a per-barrel fee (originally ~USD 1.90/bbl on West Qurna Phase 1; subsequently revised).  After a 2024 budget amendment, the compensation rate for KRG-region production was lifted from **USD 7.9/bbl to USD 16/bbl** — Iraq has been forced to sweeten terms after years of underperformance against the post-2009 service-contract template.[^21]

In recent contracting rounds (2024 5th+ Round, 6th Round) Iraq has shifted toward **profit-sharing arrangements that resemble PSCs in economic substance** — IOC takes a share of profit while the state retains 25% as royalty — but still avoids the formal PSC label, which is politically toxic in Iraq.

### Federal vs KRG split

The federal government in Baghdad and the Kurdistan Regional Government in Erbil have litigated the constitutional ownership of Kurdish hydrocarbons for two decades.  The KRG–Turkey pipeline has been **closed since March 2023** (after a Paris arbitration award against Turkey), stranding ~400,000 bpd of KRG production.  A 2024 budget amendment created a federal subsidy mechanism for KRG IOCs, but as of mid-2025 the pipeline restart remains unresolved.

### LNG potential

Iraq has effectively **zero LNG export capacity**.  Associated gas is overwhelmingly flared or reinjected.  The TotalEnergies GGIP includes an associated-gas processing component but is oriented at domestic power supply, not export.  Iraq is not a competing LNG exporter on the world market.

### Iraq fiscal stress

Per IMF Article IV (Country Report 24/128, May 2024 + 25/183, July 2025):[^23]
* 2023 fiscal balance: -1.3% of GDP (down from +8.9% in 2022)
* 2024 fiscal deficit: estimated 4.2% of GDP (and rising)
* **Breakeven oil price: ~USD 84/bbl in 2024**, up from USD 54 in 2020 — the structural wage bill has ratcheted up Iraq's break-even and made it vulnerable to even modest oil-price weakness.

Iraq is the negative-case lesson: state ownership and high nominal government take are eroded by political-economy constraints on expenditure.

## 7. The Middle East fiscal-design lesson

Across the six Middle Eastern jurisdictions surveyed — Qatar, UAE, Saudi Arabia, Oman, Algeria, Iraq — **not one uses a rent-tax design comparable to Australia's PRRT**.  The dominant fiscal architectures are:

**(a) State ownership + dividend to treasury** (Qatar via QatarEnergy; Saudi Arabia via Aramco; UAE via ADNOC; Algeria via Sonatrach; Iraq via Iraqi National Oil Company).  In all five cases, the upstream concession is held by the state or a 100%-state-owned entity, with IOCs participating only as minority JV partners or contractors.

**(b) Production-sharing or profit-sharing contracts** with state share of 56–80% of barrel/cargo after cost recovery (Algeria's PSA template; Iraq's 2024-onwards profit-sharing TSC variant).

**(c) Royalty + corporate income tax stack** at headline rates 10–35% (royalty) + 35–50% (CIT) layered on top of (a) or (b).  Saudi Aramco's effective royalty + 50% upstream CIT is the canonical example.

None of these regimes defers payment to "after cost recovery + uplift" the way Australia's PRRT does.  The PRRT design — pioneered by the Hawke government on the implicit theory that gas exploration carried so much risk and capital intensity that government should accept a back-loaded share — has produced a defensible result in petroleum (Bass Strait, where PRRT did collect material revenue from already-developed fields) but a structurally disappointing result in LNG (Gorgon, Wheatstone, Ichthys, Prelude, APLNG, GLNG, QCLNG all paid effectively zero PRRT through their first decade of production).

**The Middle East lesson is not that PRRT-style rent taxes are wrong in theory; it is that ME states have correctly diagnosed that capital-recovery-with-uplift mechanisms are inappropriate where exploration risk is low and reserves are proved.**  Qatar's North Field is the world's lowest-cost gas reservoir; Saudi's Ghawar and Safaniya are the world's lowest-cost oil; Algeria's Hassi R'Mel is similarly low-cost.  None of these states pretend that the developer needs back-loading to make the project work — they front-load revenue extraction through royalty and dividend.

This is the same conclusion the **Callaghan Review (2017)** reached for Australia: PRRT was designed for high-risk exploration economics, but the LNG buildout of 2012–2018 was substantially lower-risk than the original 1987 PRRT design contemplated, and the back-loading is therefore inappropriate.[^24]

## 8. Per-tonne LNG government take — Middle East vs Australia

This is the single most powerful comparison.  All figures in USD/tonne for clean comparison; AUD/USD = 0.66 (2024 average).

| Country | Year | Govt LNG revenue (USD bn) | LNG exports (Mt) | Govt take USD/t | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Qatar** | 2023 | ~37 (LNG-only slice of $57.5bn hydrocarbon total)[^6] | 79.7 | **~USD 465 / t** | [^4][^6] |
| **Qatar** | 2024 | ~31 (LNG-only slice of $47.5bn)[^5] | ~78 | **~USD 397 / t** | [^5][^7] |
| **Australia (all gas taxes)** | 2024-25 | 14.5 (= AUD 21.9 bn × 0.66)[^25] | ~81[^26] | **~USD 179 / t** | [^25][^26] |
| **Australia (PRRT only)** | 2024-25 | 0.89 (= AUD 1.347 bn × 0.66) | ~81 | **~USD 11 / t** | [^25] |
| **Oman LNG** | 2024 est. | UNVERIFIED (Oman LNG financials not in public domain at sufficient granularity) | 11.98[^17] | **UNVERIFIED** | [^17] |
| **Algeria** | 2024 | UNVERIFIED LNG-specific share of Sonatrach receipts | 10.7[^19] | **UNVERIFIED** | [^19] |

Three observations:

1. **Qatar extracts 2.6× as much per tonne as Australia's total gas tax take, and 36× as much as Australia's PRRT take alone.**  This is the comparison Pocock, Australia Institute, and Grattan have all centred on, and it survives independent verification against IMF and Qatari budget primary sources.

2. **Qatar's per-tonne advantage is partly explained by lower production cost** (Qatar's wellhead breakeven on North Field is widely estimated at <USD 3/MMBtu; Australia's offshore CBM-fed projects sit closer to USD 7–9/MMBtu).  Even on the same percentage royalty, Qatar's revenue-extraction headroom is larger.  But the gap is not primarily cost: it is the **share of profit accruing to the state**, which is structurally higher in Qatar because the state owns the resource and the operator.

3. **Australia's 2024-25 PRRT take of AUD 1.347 bn is approximately 0.05% of Australia's GDP, and the entire Australian oil-and-gas tax-and-royalty take is approximately 0.9% of GDP.  Qatar's hydrocarbon revenue is 81% of government revenue and approximately 19% of GDP.**  Australia is leaving an order-of-magnitude amount of revenue on the table.

## 9. Sovereign wealth fund accumulations — the long-run scorecard

The single most important measure of whether a resource-exporting state has succeeded in **converting an exhaustible resource into permanent wealth** is the sovereign wealth fund balance it has built.

| Fund | Country | AUM (USD bn, most recent) | Population (m) | AUM/capita (USD) | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Government Pension Fund Global (NBIM) | Norway | **2,131** (NOK 21,268 bn ÷ 9.98) | 5.5 | **387,000** | [^27] |
| Abu Dhabi Investment Authority (ADIA) | UAE | ~1,110 (estimate) | 10.0 | ~111,000 | [^12] |
| Kuwait Investment Authority (KIA) | Kuwait | 1,029 (March 2025) | 4.3 | **239,000** | [^28] |
| Saudi PIF | Saudi Arabia | 913 (end-2024) | 33.0 | 28,000 | [^16] |
| Qatar Investment Authority (QIA) | Qatar | 557 (Aug 2025) | 3.12 | **179,000** | [^8] |
| Mubadala | UAE (Abu Dhabi) | 385 (end-2025) | (incl. above) | n.a. | [^13] |
| Future Fund | Australia | **235** (AUD 335.3 bn ÷ 1.43) | 26.7 | **8,800** | [^29] |

The headline numbers (USD nominal): **Australia's Future Fund AUM is ~9× smaller than Norway's, and ~2.4× smaller than Qatar's QIA, despite Australia having a far larger overall economy than either.**  Australia has produced LNG at the same approximate scale as Qatar for over a decade and has nothing to show for it in equivalent permanent-wealth terms.

On a **per-capita** basis the gap is starker:
* **Norway: USD 387,000 per resident** (GPFG is funded almost exclusively from petroleum receipts via the State Direct Financial Interest + Sentralskattekontoret for Storbedrifter / Special Tax Office for Large Enterprises which administers Norway's 78% effective oil tax).
* **Australia: USD 8,800 per resident** in the Future Fund, and the Future Fund was not financed primarily from resources — it was capitalised from Telstra privatisation proceeds and consolidated revenue surpluses in 2006.  Australia has **no resource-specific sovereign wealth fund**.

The cleanest single sentence for a policy report: **"Norway took the same petroleum-export wealth Australia did and converted it into a per-capita sovereign-wealth balance 44 times the size of Australia's Future Fund."**
