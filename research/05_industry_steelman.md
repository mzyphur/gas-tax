# Industry Steelman: The Strongest Version of the Gas Industry's Case Against Higher Australian Gas Tax

**Prepared:** 2026-05-11
**Author:** Senior policy research analyst
**Purpose:** Steelman the strongest possible version of the gas industry's argument before critiquing it. This is the case the industry would make, in its own words, with its own data, presented in its strongest framing. Critique appears only in Section 9.

> **Note on verification:** Every numerical claim in Sections 1–8 is followed by the URL of the primary source and (where the source was extractable) a verbatim quote. Where a source was retrievable only through reportage (e.g. PDF blocked by Cloudflare or returned by a search engine snippet rather than the source itself), the citation flags this.

## 1. APPEA / Australian Energy Producers: Flagship Industry Claims

The peak industry body Australian Petroleum Production & Exploration Association (APPEA), rebranded as **Australian Energy Producers (AEP)** in late 2023, is the institutional voice of the case the industry asks Australians to believe.

### 1.1 Headline tax contribution figures (from AEP's most recent surveys)

AEP's July 2025 media release "Australian oil & gas industry delivers record $22 billion in taxes and royalties to government revenues in 2024-25" reports:[^1]

| Component | 2024-25 forecast | 2023-24 actual |
|---|---|---|
| Company tax | A$13.484 bn | A$12.992 bn |
| PRRT | A$1.347 bn | A$1.425 bn |
| Royalties, excise & fees | A$6.620 bn | A$6.530 bn |
| All other taxes | A$0.492 bn | A$0.591 bn |
| **Total** | **A$21.943 bn** | **A$21.538 bn** |

Headline framing, quoted: AEP CEO Samantha McCulloch claims the industry is *"the second-highest corporate taxpayer in Australia, accounting for one in every ten company tax dollars paid."*[^1]

A**three-year running total**: roughly **A$55–60 billion** across 2022-23 through 2024-25, per AEP's own running figures.[^1] [^2]

### 1.2 Headline economic-contribution figures

Per AEP, drawing on a KPMG-prepared analysis released in February 2025:[^3] [^4]
- **A$105 billion** total economic contribution (A$85 bn direct + A$20 bn indirect)
- **3.7%** of Australian GDP
- **215,000** jobs supported across the economy (**30,000** directly employed)
- **A$2.8 million** gross value added per worker (2021-22), claimed as among the highest worker productivity of any sector

### 1.3 The methodology — what's actually inside the headline number

This is the most important fact about the contribution figure, and the industry rarely volunteers it. From the AEP/KPMG framing extracted from AEP's own pages:[^1] [^3]

- **"Taxes and royalties"** = company tax + PRRT + state royalties + Commonwealth excise + payroll tax + FBT + offshore petroleum levies, **aggregated across all member companies** as **payable** (not always paid), based on a survey.
- **"$105 billion economic contribution"** is a **Computable General Equilibrium / input-output multiplier** calculation: direct industry value added plus indirect contribution (supplier purchases, induced household spending). Per ACIL Allen's parallel analysis for the broader gas economy, the multiplier methodology arrived at A$121.17 bn for the gas economy in 2021-22 — 5.25% of GDP, a 42% YoY increase that mirrors the LNG price spike.[^5]
- **"215,000 jobs"** is direct (30,000) + flow-on (185,000) under the same multiplier framework.

A separate ACIL Allen report (August 2023, commissioned by Gas Energy Australia) used the same input-output multiplier methodology to reach the $121 bn / 5.25% of GDP / 5.25× multiplier figure, confirming this is the conventional approach.[^5]

### 1.4 The "decades of upfront capex not yet paid back" argument

The industry's core defence is that current PRRT receipts are low **by design**, not by failure. As the Callaghan PRRT Review (2017) summarised the industry position verbatim (page 4, para 5):

> *"Industry argued that given the surge in investment in petroleum projects, it is inevitable that it will be some time before these projects become cash positive and pay PRRT, especially as some of these major projects have only recently commenced production or are still being developed."*[^6]

And page 4, para 8:
> *"The universal view of the petroleum industry was that the PRRT was operating as intended... Industry now considered that what some saw as a weakness in the PRRT arrangements, namely the delay in PRRT payments until projects become cash positive, was a deliberate design feature."*[^6]

## 2. The "We Paid for the Infrastructure" Argument

### 2.1 Quantum of capital invested

The industry's most factually robust claim is that Australia's LNG buildout was one of the largest private capital programmes in human history.

- APPEA / AEP figures consistently put **2010–2020 LNG capital investment at A$300–350 billion**.[^7]
- Wood Mackenzie's *LNG Taxation Estimates and Review* (April 2023) put cumulative LNG capital and operating investment in the 2010s at **USD 310 billion (≈ A$406 billion)** — broadly consistent.[^8]

### 2.2 Project-level capex (verified from public reporting)

| Project | Operator | Original sanction estimate | Final / current cost | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Gorgon LNG** | Chevron | USD 37 bn (2009 FID) | **USD 54 bn** | Australia's most expensive resource project ever; blowout driven by AUD strength, labour, logistics.[^9] |
| **Ichthys LNG** | Inpex | USD 34 bn (2012 FID) | **~USD 45 bn** | Multiple cost blow-outs; some figure disputes between Total and Inpex.[^10] |
| **Wheatstone LNG** | Chevron | USD 29 bn (2011 FID) | **USD 34 bn** | Module delivery delays; "decision to proceed when only 15% of engineering was complete."[^11] |
| **Pluto Train 2 + Scarborough** | Woodside | USD 12 bn (Nov 2021 FID) | USD 12.5 bn (≥86% complete mid-2025) | First LNG cargo targeted late 2026.[^12] |

### 2.3 The steelman of the back-loading argument

The argument is **internally coherent**:
1. PRRT taxes economic rent — i.e. returns *above* cost recovery.
2. Cost recovery on A$300+ bn of capex takes 10–20 years even on optimistic assumptions.
3. Therefore PRRT receipts in the 2010s and 2020s would always be low; rising PRRT receipts in the 2030s and 2040s are the intended outcome.
4. The Callaghan Review's own modelling at USD 65/barrel projected **~A$105 bn cumulative PRRT to 2050**, rising to **A$170 bn at USD 80/barrel** and **A$230 bn at USD 100/barrel**.[^6] (Page 11.)

### 2.4 Was Australian capex unusually high?

Yes, but the industry would argue this is **further reason to be cautious**, not less:
- Callaghan (2017) quoted industry as saying *"resource projects in Australia are 40 per cent more expensive to deliver than in the United States"* (page 6, para 4).[^6]
- Cost drivers: remote/offshore locations, deep water, high labour costs, fly-in-fly-out logistics, strong AUD during construction (~2011–2014).
- The factual case: a higher cost base means a thinner economic rent margin, meaning the PRRT base is genuinely smaller than the LNG export revenue figure suggests.

## 3. The "We Contribute Through Company Tax, Payroll, etc." Argument

### 3.1 How AEP aggregates the contribution

The industry's broader contribution claim explicitly bundles together a wide set of taxes. Per the **2023-24 figures** disclosed by Woodside (Australia's largest gas producer) in its October 2025 ATO-data media release:[^13]

**Woodside 2023-24 total: A$3.5 billion**, broken down as:
- A$2.26 bn corporate income tax
- A$796 m PRRT (largest single PRRT payer in Australia)
- Remainder: federal royalties (10–12.5% of wellhead value on NWS), federal excise (up to 30% per barrel of crude/condensate), offshore petroleum levies, **payroll tax, fringe benefits tax**.

Quoting Woodside CEO Meg O'Neill verbatim:
> *"Our Australian all-in effective tax rate for 2024 was 46%. Put simply, for every A$100 of taxable profit, A$46 of taxes apply."*[^13]

Cumulative Woodside contribution since 2011: **A$24+ billion** (includes BHP Petroleum assets from June 2022).

### 3.2 What is genuinely caused by the gas industry?

The strongest version of the industry argument is: **the gas industry creates the conditions under which these taxes flow**. Specifically:
- **Direct corporate income tax** — paid by the gas producer; clearly attributable.
- **PRRT** — paid by the project; clearly attributable.
- **State royalties + Commonwealth excise** — paid on output; clearly attributable.
- **Payroll tax / FBT** — paid by the employer on its wage bill; arguably attributable (would not exist absent the industry's employment).
- **Personal income tax of employees** — the industry includes this in its broadest aggregations. This is **less defensible** as "industry-caused tax" because workers would still pay income tax in a counterfactual employment. (See Section 9 critique.)
- **GST on supplier purchases** — same caveat.

### 3.3 The "one in every ten company tax dollars" claim

Per the ATO's 2023-24 Corporate Tax Transparency dataset, AEP states the oil & gas sector paid **A$10.4 bn in company tax** in 2023-24. AEP describes this as *"around 1 in 10 dollars of company tax paid in Australia."*[^1]

The industry-aligned source ANGEA further notes Chevron Australia paid **A$5 bn** (2024) and Woodside paid **A$4 bn** (2024) — the two largest single contributors.[^14]

## 4. The "Energy Security and Domestic Supply" Argument

### 4.1 The industry framing

The industry's case (Woodside's *Fact Checker*, AEP submissions, BCA materials) is that:
1. Australia's LNG export industry created the supply Australians now consume domestically.
2. A high windfall tax on exports would reduce future investment.
3. Less investment → less new gas → tighter domestic supply, especially in the southern states where AEMO projects shortfalls from 2028.
4. The East Coast Gas Code (July 2023) already constrains exporters via the A$12/GJ reasonable-price provision — a substantial intervention.

### 4.2 The ACCC's own data on the price gap

The factual core of the **counter**-argument is the LNG-netback / domestic-price gap. From the **ACCC East Coast Gas Inquiry June 2024 Interim Report** (verbatim):[^15]

> *"Prices offered by producers for 2024 supply in the August to December 2023 period averaged $14.32/GJ, a decrease of 2% from the preceding 6 months. Prices offered by retailers for 2024 over the same period averaged $16.51/GJ, a decrease of 16%."* (p. 48)

> *"Producer prices offered for 2025 supply averaged $15.02/GJ in the July 2023 to January 2024 period... Retailer prices offered decreased by 7% to an average of $17.79/GJ over the same period."* (p. 48)

> *"Most producer offers tracked below the short term netback prices."* (p. 67)

The East Coast Gas Code anchors a **"reasonable price" of A$12/GJ** for gas from currently operational fields; the price cap continues until at least July 2025.[^16]

### 4.3 The steelman of the supply-security argument

Even granting the netback/domestic gap, the industry's case has a defensible core:
- Australian gas reserves are not infinite; replacing depletion requires new FIDs.
- The southern states (Vic/NSW/SA) face a 24 PJ shortfall from 2028, growing every year.[^17]
- Without new offshore developments (Browse, Scarborough, Beetaloo, Narrabri), southern shortages tighten.
- Fiscal-policy uncertainty raises the hurdle rate on those FIDs.
- Therefore: **even if windfall taxes capture more rent in the short term, they may reduce supply in the medium term.**

## 5. The "Long-term Contracts and Price Exposure" Argument

### 5.1 The contract structure

A large share of Australian LNG is sold under **long-term, oil-indexed contracts** negotiated years before current spot prices. The industry argument: a windfall tax on **current** prices wrongly penalises projects whose realised revenue is materially below spot.

### 5.2 The empirical state of LNG sales

From Wood Mackenzie / IEEFA data summarised across multiple recent reports:[^18]
- **Spot/uncontracted volumes** rose to **~25% of total Australian LNG exports in 2024**.
- The remaining **~75%** is sold under long-term contracts, largely to Northeast Asian buyers, predominantly oil-indexed.
- The industry's strongest version: **a 25% export-revenue tax taxes the headline export value, not the realised contract price**, which can be materially below spot.

Budget 2026-27 partly validates the contract-structure point while also showing its fiscal limit. Treasury says PRRT receipts have been revised up by $400m in 2026-27 and $1.6bn over the five years to 2029-30, primarily because of higher near-term oil prices and higher production volumes. It also notes that most Australian LNG exports are sold under long-term contracts linked to oil prices.[^27] In other words, oil linkage can lift PRRT when oil prices rise, but the lift in the current Budget is modest relative to LNG export value and does not remove the case for structural design reform.

### 5.3 The counter-fact, fairly stated

In 2022, when spot LNG (JKM) traded at extreme levels (USD 30–70/MMBtu), spot sales were a substantial share of the windfall. Industry argues the 2022 spike was anomalous; a permanent windfall tax based on a one-year anomaly is poor policy design.

## 6. The "We'll Move Investment Elsewhere" Argument

### 6.1 The industry's claim

Tax goes up → investment leaves. This is the central political threat from industry submissions.

### 6.2 The Norway counter-example

Norway operates the world's most aggressive petroleum tax regime — a **78% combined marginal rate** (22% ordinary corporate + 71.8% special petroleum tax, designed for combined effective rate of 78%).[^19]

Despite this:
- Equinor's organic capex was **USD 10.2 bn in 2023**, **USD 13.5 bn in 2024**, with USD 10.6 bn projected for 2025.[^20]
- Norway's projected 2025 petroleum-sector investment: **NOK ~275 bn (≈ USD 24.6 bn)**.[^20]
- Equinor will drill **26 wildcat/appraisal wells off Norway in 2026**, with plans for **250 exploration wells over the next decade**.[^21]
- The Norwegian system is **neutral by design**: cash-flow taxation with immediate deduction for investment, ensuring *"an investment project that is profitable before tax is also profitable after tax."*[^19]

### 6.3 The UK Energy Profits Levy counter-counter-example

Industry typically cites the UK as showing that windfall taxes destroy investment. The empirical record is more mixed:
- The UK's EPL was raised to **38%** in November 2024, bringing total tax burden to **78%** (matching Norway).[^22]
- UK exploration hit a **record low of 4 wells drilled in 2024**.[^22]
- Some analysts (Stifel) warn the EPL has *"undermined the UK's energy security and deterred future investment"* and that the OBR has overestimated tax receipts through 2030 by **£10 bn**.[^22]

The honest steelman: **headline rate is not the full story**. Norway pairs 78% with cash-flow deductions and immediate write-off; UK paired 78% with **removal** of the 29% investment allowance. The same headline rate produces different results depending on deduction structure.

### 6.4 Australia-specific evidence

Post-2023 PRRT reforms (the 90% deductions cap, see Section 7):
- Woodside took **FID on Scarborough/Pluto Train 2 in Nov 2021** before reforms; project >86% complete by mid-2025, first cargo late 2026.[^12]
- North West Shelf life extended to 2070 (approved 2024–25).[^12]
- ExxonMobil-Woodside Turrum Phase 3 FID approved 2025 (A$350 m).[^12]
- Browse remains in regulatory limbo, with industry citing fiscal and approvals uncertainty.
- Santos's Narrabri FID delayed to 2025+.[^12]

The industry's strongest case: there is no clean Australian counterfactual to test the windfall-tax hypothesis — but the **direction of travel** (Browse delay, marginal projects deferred) is consistent with the industry argument.

## 7. The "Australia's PRRT Was Deliberately Designed This Way" Argument

This is the industry's strongest argument from a tax-policy-design perspective.

### 7.1 PRRT's design intent (1987 onwards)

The PRRT, legislated 1987 and effective 1988, was designed to capture **economic rent** — i.e. returns above those *"necessary to attract commercial investment into the activity"* — after cost recovery.[^6] (Callaghan, page 4.) Crucially, it was designed to avoid the *"problems of an excise or royalty regime which are considered to discourage activity and investment in marginal projects."*[^6]

The PRRT is therefore **back-loaded by design**. Quoting Callaghan verbatim (page 10):
> *"Under the PRRT arrangements, tax only becomes payable once projects become cash flow positive, meaning all expenditure has been deducted."*[^6]

### 7.2 The Callaghan Review (2017) — the most authoritative independent review

Callaghan was commissioned by the Turnbull government specifically to answer whether PRRT is working as designed. Its key conclusions, verbatim:[^6]

**On "is PRRT working?":**
> *"Given the magnitude of the investment in Australia's petroleum industry over the past decade, it is evident that the PRRT is not discouraging investment in exploration and development of Australia's petroleum resources."* (page 10, para 1.)

> *"The fact that PRRT revenue has been declining and is not rising in line with the increase in LNG production does not of itself indicate that the Australian community is not receiving an equitable return from the development of its resources."* (page 10, para 3.)

**On long-term revenue projections (at USD 65/barrel):**
> *"PRRT revenue could total around $12 billion in the next 10 years (to 2027) and around $105 billion in total over the period to 2050."* (page 11.)

**At higher prices:**
- USD 80/barrel: **A$25 bn to 2027; A$170 bn to 2050.**
- USD 100/barrel: **A$45 bn to 2027; A$230 bn to 2050.**

**On the "no economic rent in LNG" argument:**
> *"The argument that there are no economic rents in LNG projects, and as such the PRRT is not appropriate for such projects, appears to be based on the assumption that oil and gas prices will largely remain at low levels over the life of current projects."* (page 12.)

**Crucially, Callaghan's overall finding** (page 13):
> *"The PRRT remains the preferred way to achieve a fair return to the community for the extraction of petroleum resources without discouraging investment, [but] changes should be made to PRRT arrangements to make them more compatible with the developments that have taken place in the Australian oil and gas industry."*[^6]

And on retrospectivity (page 13):
> *"Any major change to the design of the PRRT should only apply to new projects."*[^6]

### 7.3 The Henry Review (2010)

The Henry Tax Review's broader framework also endorsed taxation of economic rent from natural resources as one of four "efficient tax bases."[^23] Its recommendation: a **40% uniform resource rent tax** — the same rate as the existing PRRT.[^23] In other words, the Henry Review's recommended rate is the rate **already legislated for PRRT**.

The industry can credibly argue: the design is endorsed by Australia's most comprehensive modern tax review.

### 7.4 The 2023 PRRT reforms (already implemented)

The Albanese government, accepting many Callaghan recommendations, legislated a **90% deductions cap** effective 1 July 2023:[^24]
- Limits the proportion of PRRT assessable income that can be offset by deductions to **90%**.
- Applies to LNG projects 7 years after first production (or from 1 July 2023, whichever later).
- Treasury revenue estimate: **+A$2.4 bn over forward estimates**; ~A$7 bn additional PRRT to 2033-34.

The industry's argument: **substantial reform has already happened**. The Pocock 25% proposal is a *third* round of fiscal change on assets that have already absorbed Callaghan-derived reforms.

## 8. The Industry's Preferred Reform Package

### 8.1 What AEP / Woodside / Chevron actually want

The industry's de facto preferred package is:
1. **No new export tax.** A 25% export-revenue tax (Pocock / Greens / Australia Institute proposal) is treated as fundamentally incompatible with PRRT design.
2. **No retrospective changes** to existing PRRT arrangements (consistent with Callaghan's "only new projects" recommendation).
3. **Stability and predictability** — even if the rate stays the same, repeated reviews depress investment.
4. **Faster approvals**, not more tax. Browse, Beetaloo, and Narrabri are the projects whose timelines they want compressed.
5. **Recognition of the 2023 reforms** as having already resolved the back-loading issue.

### 8.2 The industry's empirical critique of the 25% proposal

Quoting the framing used by Woodside, AEP, and ANGEA:[^14]
- A 25% revenue tax taxes **gross revenue**, not profit — incompatible with PRRT's economic-rent design.
- Many long-term contracts are oil-indexed at prices set years ago; a revenue tax would tax notional prices that aren't being received.
- The 2023 PRRT cap already moves revenue forward; layering a revenue tax on top is double-counting.
- AEP-commissioned modelling (Wood Mackenzie, 2023) argued the existing tax system would deliver **A$80 bn more tax under higher oil-price scenarios** — i.e. the system is responsive to prices.[^8]

### 8.3 Industry-commissioned consulting reports

The industry case is supported (and produced) by a body of consulting work:
- **PwC (2014):** *Value-adding: Australian Oil and Gas Industry* — input-output multiplier methodology for direct/indirect contribution.[^25]
- **Deloitte Access Economics (2012, 2018, 2024):** Multiple economic-contribution analyses for APPEA.[^25]
- **EY (2020):** *Australia's oil and gas industry: kickstarting recovery from COVID-19* — projected A$350 bn of national economic output gain and 220,000 jobs over 20 years under a "high growth" scenario.[^26]
- **EY (2021):** *The economic contribution of Queensland's oil and gas industry*.[^25]
- **KPMG (Feb 2025):** The current AEP economic-contribution analysis underpinning the A$105 bn / 215,000 jobs claim.[^3]
- **ACIL Allen (Aug 2023):** *The economic contribution of the Australian gas economy in 2021-22* — A$121.17 bn / 5.25% of GDP.[^5]
- **Wood Mackenzie (April 2023):** *LNG Taxation Estimates and Review* — cumulative USD 310 bn / A$406 bn investment in 2010s.[^8]

These reports share a **methodological backbone**: input-output / CGE multipliers, member-survey data for tax/royalty figures, project-level capex from operator disclosures. The methodology is **internally consistent** and reproducible, even if (as critics argue) the multipliers and the boundary of "contribution" are contestable.

## 9. An Honest Assessment

This is the analyst's evaluation. The industry would not write Section 9 — it is included to honour the brief's commitment to fair representation followed by honest critique.

### 9.1 What is genuinely strong in the industry's case

1. **The PRRT is back-loaded by design, not by failure.** This is not industry spin; it is the explicit design intent confirmed by the Callaghan Review (2017) and grounded in Henry Review (2010) principles. A serious policy debate must engage with the design choice, not pretend it was an accident.

2. **Australian capex was genuinely massive.** A$300+ bn of private capital in the 2010s built an industry that did not exist before. The Gorgon, Wheatstone, Ichthys, and APLNG capex blow-outs were real and shrunk realised rents materially.

3. **Headline LNG prices ≠ realised prices.** A 25% **revenue** tax conflates the headline export value with what producers actually receive. Roughly 75% of Australian LNG sells under long-term oil-indexed contracts, with realised prices materially below spot in high-price years.

4. **Norway's example cuts both ways.** Norway proves a 78% rate **can** coexist with strong investment — but only if paired with a cash-flow tax design and immediate deduction. A naive 25% revenue tax in Australia is not equivalent to the Norwegian system.

5. **The industry pays substantial company tax and royalties.** The A$21.9 bn / 2024-25 figure is real, even if its composition is more flattering than the "PRRT alone" figure.

6. **The 2023 PRRT reforms are already changing the revenue profile.** The 90% deductions cap was a substantive reform — the policy debate has moved on from "PRRT is broken" to "is PRRT enough?".

### 9.2 What is weak or misleading

1. **The "$105 bn economic contribution" headline mixes apples and oranges.** It bundles direct value-added (defensible) with input-output multiplier "indirect" and "induced" contributions (much weaker theoretically — multipliers typically assume idle resources and fixed prices, conditions that do not hold in a high-employment Australian economy). Workers' personal income tax is included in some aggregations, which is **double counting**: those workers would (mostly) be employed elsewhere and pay income tax there.

2. **"One in 10 company tax dollars" is correct but uninformative on the rent question.** Company tax is paid on profit at 30% — same as every other Australian corporate. The relevant question for the gas tax debate is **resource rent**, not company tax.

3. **The Callaghan modelling assumes USD 65/barrel oil**, set in 2016–17. Actual realised oil prices have averaged considerably higher across multiple years since (notably 2022). The "PRRT will eventually deliver" claim relied on price assumptions the industry itself often argues are too conservative — but Callaghan's scenarios scale linearly, and the high-price scenario (USD 100/barrel, A$230 bn to 2050) is plausible given the post-2021 price record.

4. **The investment-flight argument is structurally weaker than the industry claims.** The UK EPL case cited is contaminated by the removal of investment allowances. The Norway case shows that **a well-designed high-rate regime preserves investment.** The industry cannot have it both ways — either headline rate matters (in which case Norway falsifies the claim) or design matters (in which case Australia's PRRT reform debate is the right frame, not "any new tax kills investment").

5. **The "we built the industry with our own money" argument elides ownership of the underlying resource.** The capex is real, but the gas is owned by the Australian community in the constitutional sense. The PRRT design is explicitly the mechanism for rent-sharing once capex is recovered. The industry argument that "we own this because we paid the capex" misrepresents the design.

6. **Inconsistent treatment of "the system is working" vs "the system is fragile."** When defending the tax record, the industry cites A$21.9 bn / year as proof of contribution. When opposing reform, the industry cites investment fragility and the need for fiscal stability. Both claims may be true, but they sit in tension.

### 9.3 Where industry and Australia Institute / Pocock genuinely disagree

The underlying disagreement is **not about facts** but about **policy frame**:

| Question | Industry frame | Pocock / TAI frame |
|---|---|---|
| What is the tax base? | Economic rent (PRRT design) | Export revenue, like state royalties |
| When should tax be paid? | After cost recovery (back-loaded) | From production (early, assured) |
| Whose interest is primary? | Investor certainty / fiscal stability | Community rent capture / "fair share" |
| What is the right rate? | The existing 40% PRRT on rent | 25% on gross export revenue |
| What's wrong with current system? | Nothing fundamental — 2023 reforms addressed concerns | LNG projects pay no PRRT despite huge exports |
| Reference international system | Norway (high rate but neutral design) | Qatar / Russia / Indonesia (revenue-based regimes) |
| Risk frame | Investment flight, supply shortfall | Stranded asset / decarbonisation, lost public revenue |

**The genuine factual disagreement** is narrower than the rhetorical disagreement. Both sides agree:
- Australia exports ~75–80 mtpa of LNG worth A$80–100 bn/year.
- Current PRRT receipts are ~A$1.3–1.4 bn/year.
- Most existing LNG projects will not pay material PRRT until the 2030s.
- The A$300+ bn capex programme is real.

They disagree on **what those facts mean** and **what policy should follow**. The intellectually honest debate is about resource-ownership philosophy, tax-design theory, and risk-weighting under uncertainty — not about the numerical facts.
