## 2. Share of PRRT from oil vs. LNG

The Callaghan Review (2017) is unambiguous: **the PRRT was designed for the Bass Strait oil province, not for LNG**, and PRRT has tracked oil — not gas — production through the entire LNG-boom era.

Direct quote from Callaghan, Chapter 2: "PRRT collections have moved roughly in line with oil production, but have yet to respond to increasing gas production"[^22] (Figure 2.2, p. 39).

The Callaghan Review modelled future PRRT take. At $US65/barrel, "PRRT revenue could total around $12 billion in the next 10 years (to 2027)"[^23] — i.e. averaging just $1.2bn/year. Actual FY2017-18 to FY2024-25 receipts totalled **$10.06 billion**, hitting that modelled trajectory almost exactly even though Brent averaged well above $65/bbl across the period.

The Australia Institute's reconciliation (*A Stronger PRRT Cap*, May 2024) finds the disconnect between gas production and PRRT receipts began with three specific events: "the Gladstone LNG project entered operation. ... that also occurred with the beginning of the Ichthys LNG project in July 2018 and the shipping of the first LNG cargo from the Prelude floating LNG facility in 2019"[^24]. Before those three projects came online, PRRT was correlated with both gas and oil revenue; after, gas industry revenue and tax revenue split entirely.

**The 2019 reform conclusion (Callaghan) and 2023 reform (Chalmers):**

- Callaghan recommended tightening, but did **not** recommend a deductions cap. Treasury rejected a deductions cap option at the time.
- The 2023 reform (announced May 2023 Budget, legislated 2024, effective 1 July 2024 for instalments) **does cap deductions at 90% of assessable receipts** for LNG projects — i.e. requires LNG projects to recognise 10% taxable income regardless of carried-forward deductions[^25][^26].
- Treasury's 2023 Budget estimate: the cap would raise **$2.4 billion over the forward estimates** (4 years to 2026-27)[^27].
- **By MYEFO 2023-24 (December 2023)** — seven months later — Treasury revised PRRT receipts down by "$0.8 billion in 2023–24 and $2.4 billion over the four years to 2026–27"[^28]. The Australia Institute's interpretation: "even though the oil price has risen since the changes were announced, the new regime will collect no new extra revenue"[^28].
- The actual 2023-24 outcome was $1,144m receipts vs. the original Budget 2023-24 forecast of ~$2.45bn[^29] — i.e. **the cap's "first effect" year came in roughly half** of what was claimed at announcement.

## 3. Why PRRT collects so little from LNG: the deductions backlog

### 3a. The deduction stockpile

The Callaghan Review's Figure 2.3 ("Total deductible expenditure") shows the carried-forward deduction stockpile across the entire industry climbing from ~$50bn in 2002 to **~$300bn by 2015-16** (the chart's y-axis explicitly reaches $300,000m)[^30]. This is roughly **3× annual LNG export earnings** at the time, and growing every year because each unused deduction is uplifted forward.

The widely-cited *$238bn* figure for "PRRT credits" (Australia Institute, Tax Justice Network, multiple Senate inquiry submissions) is the ATO's estimate of carried-forward exploration and general project expenditure as of the mid-2010s; the Callaghan Figure 2.3 series shows it crossed $238bn around 2014.

### 3b. Uplift rates: how a deduction grows in the dark

PRRT carries forward unused expenditure with a compounding "uplift" each year. From Callaghan §4.2[^31]:

- **1988–1990:** Exploration uplift = LTBR + 15 percentage points; general project uplift = LTBR + 15 pp.
- **1990 onwards:** General project uplift reduced to LTBR + 5 pp (when transferability of exploration expenditure between projects was introduced). Exploration uplift stayed at **LTBR + 15 pp** — even though transferability *reduced* the risk that the deduction would not be used.
- Exploration spending older than 5 years before a production licence: uplifted at the GDP factor (lower).
- Plus "ordering of deductions" — the schedule that says lower-uplift deductions get used first, so the higher-uplift exploration deductions sit and compound for years.

Callaghan summary: "Industry argued that the uplift rate reflected the high risks associated with exploration. However, exploration expenditure that takes place more than five years before a production licence is issued, and is arguably more risky, is only uplifted at the GDP factor rate."[^31] In other words the *most* risky exploration spend gets the *lower* uplift, and the *less* risky later-stage spend gets the *higher* uplift — exactly backwards from any defensible risk-pricing rationale.

The Australia Institute (*A Stronger PRRT Cap*, p. 9-10): "Only when the cumulative revenue has exceeded both the operating expenses and the compounded capital outlays is there deemed to be a taxable profit ... [this] works against taxing rents early in the life of a project, and instead commonly sees PRRT applied at the precise point at which deductions are much higher than revenue so that super profits are unlikely to be taxed."[^32]

### 3c. Project-level deduction shields

The Australia Institute's 2017 Senate inquiry submission (reflected in *The Conversation*, May 2017) gave the bluntest project list: "Five new offshore gas projects are coming online: Gorgon, Wheatstone, Ichthys, Pluto and Prelude. When these are running at full production capacity they are unlikely to pay any PRRT for many years to come – the companies themselves concede it will be 2029 – and no royalties apply."[^33]

The accumulated-deduction position by major project, from public submissions and company disclosures:

- **Gorgon (Chevron-operated, Shell/ExxonMobil partners):** Construction cost blown out from ~A$37bn at FID (2009) to ~A$80bn+. Effectively all of that is deductible expenditure carried forward with uplift. Chevron itself confirmed it operated "in a tax loss until 2021" because "Chevron spent more than $80 billion from 2009-2018 constructing the Gorgon and Wheatstone natural gas projects"[^34].
- **Wheatstone (Chevron-operated):** Same vintage, similar capital structure to Gorgon. Chevron is the lead PRRT-affected entity in both.
- **Ichthys (INPEX-operated):** First LNG cargo July 2018. Capital cost A$45bn. INPEX paid $0 in PRRT through FY2023-24[^35].
- **Prelude (Shell):** First cargo 2019. Shell's own filings have indicated the project "will never pay PRRT" — quoted by *The Guardian* via Australia Institute[^36].
- **Pluto (Woodside):** First LNG 2012. Has paid no PRRT to date and is not expected to in the near term.
- **NWS (Woodside-operated JV — BHP, Shell, BP, Chevron, MIMI):** This is the *oldest* offshore LNG project and pays Commonwealth Royalty in lieu of PRRT for some of its production (until 2012 when PRRT was extended to NWS). NWS is the **only** offshore LNG project that has historically paid Commonwealth resource revenue at scale, and that revenue is shrinking with the field's decline (the FBO line "Petroleum royalty" — distinct from PRRT — was $992m in 2019-20[^37]).

## 6. The 2023 PRRT reforms (deductions cap)

### 6a. What changed

The mechanism (legislated by the Treasury Laws Amendment (Tax Accountability and Fairness) Act 2024[^25] and the PRRT Assessment Regulations 2024[^26]):

- For LNG projects: total deductions in a year are **capped at 90% of assessable receipts**.
- Effective for income years beginning on or after 1 July 2023 (i.e. FY2023-24 onwards).
- A 7-year grace period: projects are not subject to the cap until 7 years after first production, or 1 July 2023, whichever is later[^25].
- Excess deductions blocked by the cap are not lost; they are carried forward with the (lower) LTBR uplift.
- Quarterly PRRT instalment regime starts FY2024-25[^26].

### 6b. Treasury's revenue estimates — original vs revised

**At 2023-24 Budget (May 2023):**
- Treasury estimated the reform would raise an additional **$2.4 billion over the forward estimates** (4 years to 2026-27)[^27]. The Treasurer's second-reading speech cited this number; the Australian Energy Producers (ex-APPEA) immediately called for "bipartisan support" — i.e. the industry's preferred option of the three Treasury had on the table[^27].

**By MYEFO 2023-24 (December 2023):**
- "Petroleum resource rent tax receipts have been revised down by $0.8 billion in 2023–24 and $2.4 billion over the four years to 2026–27"[^28]. In aggregate the entire $2.4bn "lift" announced 7 months earlier had been re-rated to zero — *even though Treasury was projecting higher oil prices than at Budget*.

**At 2024-25 Budget (May 2024):**
- PRRT receipts forecast at $2,650m for FY2024-25 (one year ahead) and $2,050m / $1,850m / $1,750m for FY25-26 / 26-27 / 27-28[^19]. So Treasury restored some of the upward path, mostly via oil-price assumptions.

**At 2025-26 Budget (March 2025):**
- Forecasts cut again: FY2025-26 $1,950m, FY2026-27 $1,800m, FY2027-28 $1,650m, FY2028-29 $1,450m[^13].
- The 2024-25 actual outcome ($1,420m receipts vs $2,650m projected) is **45% below** the May 2024 forecast.
- *Sydney Morning Herald* / Australia Institute analysis: "the tweaked PRRT would raise $4 billion less over the forward estimates than the government had said in 2023."[^51]

**At Budget 2026-27 (May 2026):**
- Forecasts moved to FY2025-26 $1,400m, FY2026-27 $1,900m, FY2027-28 $1,750m, FY2028-29 $1,550m and FY2029-30 $1,250m.[^54]
- Treasury explicitly attributes the near-term PRRT upgrade to higher oil prices and production volumes, noting that most Australian LNG exports are sold through long-term contracts linked to oil prices.[^54]
- The update improves the 2026-27 point estimate, but does not alter the structural diagnosis: the forward path still fades, and beer excise remains above PRRT in every year of the forward estimates.

### 6c. What independent analysts say

- **Australia Institute** (*A Stronger PRRT Cap*, May 2024)[^52]: Proposed alternative caps at 80% (additional ~$1bn/year vs the 90% cap) or 60% (~$2-3bn/year). Modelled estimates: 90% cap $0.5bn-$0.8bn/year additional; 80% cap $1.0bn-$1.6bn/year; 60% cap $2.0bn-$3.3bn/year over FY2023-24 to FY2026-27. **Even the 60% cap was a "very small sum compared to projected LNG exports earnings."**
- **IEEFA** has consistently argued the cap is symbolic and that a meaningful PRRT requires either a much tighter cap or a switch to a Commonwealth royalty on all offshore LNG production (parallel to the existing NWS royalty regime).
- **Crikey** (Bernard Keane, May 2023): "Petroleum resource rent tax revenue boost is PR spin" — argued at announcement that the $2.4bn figure was contingent on oil-price assumptions that would themselves drive PRRT receipts higher regardless[^53].

### 6d. What actually came in

- FY2023-24 (first year of the cap, applied at returns lodgement only — instalments started a year later): PRRT receipts **$1,144m**, *down* from $2,287m in FY2022-23 and well below the Budget 2023-24 forecast of ~$2.45bn cited by the Australia Institute[^28][^14]. Some of that gap is the oil-price unwind, not the cap's fault — but the cap was supposed to substantially offset the oil-price drop, and clearly did not.
- FY2024-25 (first year of cap-driven instalments): PRRT receipts **$1,420m**[^15] — slightly above 2023-24 but still **far below** the $2,650m the May 2024 Budget had projected[^19], and only $276m above FY2023-24.
- The "5 new PRRT payers" in the FY2023-24 ATO Corporate Transparency data[^40] are the cap's most visible effect. Even so, total $$$ collected fell.
