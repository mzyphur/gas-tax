# International Comparison — Australia's Gas Export Tax Regime vs Major LNG Exporters

**File:** `04_international_comparison.md`
**Date compiled:** 11 May 2026
**Author:** Senior international fiscal policy analyst (research dossier for Australian policy report on gas export taxation)

---

## Executive summary

Australia is one of the world's two largest LNG exporters by volume but is, by a wide margin, the largest LNG exporter that fails to translate that volume into commensurate state revenue. In 2023-24 the Petroleum Resource Rent Tax (PRRT) — the sole Commonwealth instrument designed to capture economic rent from offshore gas — yielded just **A$1.483 billion** in payable tax across 16 entities,[^1] while the Australian government collected **A$5.1 billion** from HECS/HELP student loan repayments in the same year — more than four times PRRT receipts.[^2]

Compared head-to-head against Norway — the only other OECD LNG exporter with a comparable resource base, a comparable level of legal and contractual sophistication, and a comparable position as a high-trust, market-economy gas supplier to allies — Australia collects roughly two orders of magnitude less petroleum revenue per capita. Norway's 2024 state net cash flow from petroleum was **NOK 702.2 billion** (approximately **USD 65 billion**)[^3] from a population of **5.63 million**.[^4] Australia's combined federal PRRT plus NWS royalty share for 2023-24 was approximately **A$2.1 billion** (PRRT A$1.483 bn[^1] plus net federal NWS royalty share, with the Commonwealth's gross take c. A$367 million in recent years[^5]) from a population of **27.72 million**.[^6]

The mechanism for Australia's underperformance is well-understood and structural, not coincidental: a profit-based PRRT with carry-forward uplifts that allowed most offshore LNG mega-projects to defer or eliminate tax liability for decades; no royalty on Commonwealth offshore waters (other than NWS legacy fields); no production-sharing component; no state-equity stake; and no sovereign wealth fund to compound retained rents. Every comparator country in this dossier collects more state revenue per tonne of LNG exported than Australia does, in most cases by a factor of 5 to 50.

## 1) Headline comparison — tax revenue per unit of gas exported

Government petroleum revenue is not directly comparable across jurisdictions because the legal mechanisms differ: Norway uses a 78% special tax, Qatar uses state ownership through QatarEnergy, the US uses royalties plus state severance plus corporate income tax, the UK layers an Energy Profits Levy on top of Ring Fence Corporation Tax, and Malaysia/Indonesia use production-sharing contracts with state-owned operators. To make the comparison meaningful I have used **total government petroleum (oil + gas) net revenue** as the numerator and **most recent year's LNG export volume** as one denominator, plus **population** as a second denominator for the per-capita view. Petroleum-revenue figures include both oil and gas where they cannot be cleanly separated; this overstates the "LNG-specific" take for diversified producers like Norway, the US and the UK, and the comparison should be read as conservative (i.e., it understates how badly Australia performs).

This is the central, defensible quantitative finding of this dossier. The remainder of the document explains why.

## 2) Norway — the gold standard

### 2.1 The tax regime

Norway operates a two-stage corporate petroleum tax: a 22% ordinary corporate income tax plus a 71.8% special petroleum tax, structured so that paid corporate tax is written off against the special-tax base, producing a **combined marginal rate of 78%** on petroleum profits.[^18] The system is explicitly designed to be neutral — investments yielding positive net present value before tax should also yield positive NPV after tax — and as of income year 2022 the special tax operates as a cash-flow tax with immediate 100% deduction of investments rather than depreciation.[^18] Losses are fully consolidable across the continental shelf, not ring-fenced at project level (the opposite of Australia's PRRT, which ring-fences losses to the project that incurred them).

Transfer-pricing risk — the issue that has crippled the PRRT for integrated Australian LNG projects — is addressed by the **Petroleum Price Council**, which sets reference (norm) prices for crude and gas sales between affiliated entities, removing the gas-transfer-pricing problem at source.[^18]

In addition to the 78% profit tax, Norway captures rent through:
- **State's Direct Financial Interest (SDFI)** — a portfolio of direct state ownership stakes in producing fields, generating net cash flow that bypasses corporate tax entirely
- **Equinor dividend** — the Norwegian state owns ~67% of Equinor and receives that share of all dividends
- **Environmental taxes** including a carbon tax on offshore gas of **NOK 2.21 per Sm³** in 2025 and a natural-gas emissions tax of **NOK 20.17 per Sm³** in 2025[^19]
- **Area fees** on petroleum exploration and production licences

### 2.2 Revenue collected

Norway's state net cash flow from petroleum activities:

| Year | State net cash flow (NOK bn) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 2022 | 1,457[^20] | Highest in history; reflects post-Ukraine-invasion gas price spike |
| 2023 | 978[^20] | Includes NOK 474 bn in direct company-tax payments[^20] |
| **2024** | **702.2**[^21] | Statsbudsjettet 2026 Meld. St. 1 (2025-2026) Table 3.5 page 28 — verified primary-source actual figure; supersedes the earlier Statsbudsjettet 2025 (Oct 2024) estimate of NOK 680 bn |
| 2025 (est.) | 656[^19] | Comprising NOK 373.1 bn taxes, NOK 238.9 bn SDFI, NOK 34.6 bn Equinor dividend, NOK 9.2 bn env. tax / area fees[^19] |
| 2026 (forecast) | 521[^19] | NOK 291.5 bn taxes, NOK 193 bn SDFI, NOK 25.8 bn Equinor dividend, NOK 11 bn env. tax[^19] |

At Q4 2025 prevailing exchange rates (NOK ~10.8 / USD), NOK 702.2 bn is approximately **USD 65 bn** for 2024 alone. Across the past three years (2022–24), Norway has captured approximately **USD 290 billion** from oil and gas activities, against gross export earnings that — while substantial — are not three times larger than Australia's. The take-share differential, not the gross-revenue differential, drives the per-capita gap.

### 2.3 The Government Pension Fund Global (the oil fund)

All petroleum revenue net cash flow is transferred to the **Government Pension Fund Global** (Statens pensjonsfond utland), managed by Norges Bank Investment Management. The fiscal rule restricts annual transfers from fund to budget to the expected real return — **currently estimated at 3%** — meaning that the principal is never depleted and the spending stream is intergenerational.[^22]

As at end-2025, the fund's market value was approximately **NOK 21,300 billion** (≈ **USD 2.0 trillion**), equivalent to roughly NOK 3.8 million per registered Norwegian resident.[^23][^24] An earlier Q3 2025 valuation reported by CNBC was approximately USD 2.0 trillion; Wikipedia records USD 1.77 trillion as at November 2024.[^25] Either reading establishes the fund as the world's largest single sovereign wealth fund.

### 2.4 Norway's government take per dollar of petroleum revenue

If one combines the 78% marginal profit tax, the SDFI direct-equity dividends, and the Equinor dividend share, the consolidated state take on a marginal barrel/cubic metre of Norwegian petroleum has been estimated by NPD and Ministry of Finance documents at approximately **80%–85% of net resource rent**. The Australian comparator — combining the PRRT's nominal 40% rate with carry-forward uplifts and project-level ring-fencing — has been shown in independent IEEFA and Australia Institute analysis to capture a single-digit percentage of equivalent rent over the life of an offshore LNG project.[^26]

This is the headline differential.

## 3) Qatar — market power, state ownership, no separate tax

Qatar's fiscal architecture differs from Norway's because the production company **QatarEnergy** (formerly Qatar Petroleum) is itself a state entity. Revenue flows directly to the Qatari treasury through dividends, state royalties, and corporate income tax on partner companies (typically the international oil companies in joint ventures). The IMF treats hydrocarbon revenue as a single line in Qatar's fiscal accounts.

### 3.1 Production and exports

- **2024 LNG production capacity:** 77 Mt/y[^27]
- **2030 target capacity:** 142 Mt/y (an 85% increase via North Field East, South, and West expansion phases)[^27][^28]
- **Market share goal:** ~25% of global LNG by end of decade[^27]

### 3.2 Government revenue from hydrocarbons

- **2022 hydrocarbon export revenue:** USD 115 bn (post-Ukraine peak)[^11]
- **2023 hydrocarbon export revenue:** USD 85 bn[^11]
- **2023 total Qatari public revenues:** QR 254.4 bn (USD 69.9 bn)[^29]
- **Hydrocarbon share of total government revenue, 2023:** **83%**[^11]
- **2023 fiscal surplus:** 5½% of GDP[^11]
- **2023 current account surplus:** 17% of GDP[^11]

### 3.3 Qatar Investment Authority (sovereign wealth fund)

- **2024 AUM estimate:** ~USD 475 bn[^30]
- **August 2025 estimate:** ~USD 557 bn[^30]
- QIA was founded in 2005 specifically to diversify the state's hydrocarbon dependency

### 3.4 Per-capita view

Qatar's resident population in 2024 was approximately **2.74 million** (88.4% expatriate, 11.6% Qatari national).[^31] Allocating 2023's USD 85 bn hydrocarbon export revenue across the full resident population produces a **per-capita figure of approximately USD 31,000**. Restricted to citizens only (~318,000 nationals), the per-citizen figure exceeds **USD 267,000 per year** — a number which is the basis of Qatar's universal-citizen welfare state, free utilities, free healthcare, free university, no income tax, and one of the highest GDP-per-capita figures in the world.

## 4) USA — federal royalties + state severance + corporate income tax

The US has no resource-rent tax and no state-equity participation. Federal revenue from oil and gas flows through three channels:

### 4.1 Offshore federal royalties (Outer Continental Shelf)

- Historical OCS royalty rate: **12.5% of value at the wellhead** on legacy leases; **16.67%** for new leases under post-2022 rules; the Inflation Reduction Act raised statutory minimum to **16.67%** with a ceiling of 18.75%[^32]
- **FY2024 OCS oil and gas revenue:** **USD 7.0 billion** in royalties, bonuses and rents[^8]
- FY2024 OCS production: ~668 million bbl oil + ~700 Bcf gas[^8]

### 4.2 Onshore federal royalties

- Historical onshore federal royalty rate: **12.5%**; increased to **16.67%** for new leases under IRA-era reforms
- Distinct from state severance taxes which apply on top

### 4.3 State severance taxes

- Texas, Louisiana, Alaska, Wyoming, Oklahoma each impose their own severance taxes — typical range 4.6%–12.25% of value, with credits and exemptions for marginal production
- Texas natural-gas severance tax: 7.5% of market value
- Louisiana: 12.5 cents per Mcf
- These are administered state-by-state and are not aggregated in any single federal table

### 4.4 Total Interior Department disbursements FY2024

The US Department of the Interior reported total energy revenue disbursements of **USD 16.45 billion** in FY2024 across all energy and mineral commodities and all federal lands (onshore plus offshore plus Tribal):[^9]

- USD 6.32 bn to U.S. Treasury
- USD 4.29 bn to states
- USD 3.09 bn to the Reclamation Fund
- USD 1.18 bn to Tribes and individual Indian mineral owners
- USD 1.01 bn to the Land and Water Conservation Fund
- USD 0.41 bn to federal agencies
- USD 0.15 bn to the Historic Preservation Fund

### 4.5 LNG export surge — scale context

- 2014 US LNG exports: 16,253 MMcf[^33]
- 2015 US LNG exports: 32,382 MMcf[^33]
- 2020 US LNG exports: 2,389,614 MMcf (147× the 2014 baseline)[^33]
- 2024 US LNG exports: 4,367,171 MMcf, equivalent to 11.9 Bcf/d, approximately **88.3 Mt**[^7][^33]

The US went from a negligible LNG exporter to the world's largest in under a decade. Despite that growth, federal revenue capture per tonne remains modest by international standards because the regime relies on a low royalty rate plus standard corporate income tax, with no rent-tax overlay.

## 5) UK North Sea — Ring Fence CT + Supplementary Charge + EPL

The UK regime layers multiple instruments on petroleum profits:

- **Ring Fence Corporation Tax (RFCT):** 30% (ring-fenced to UK Continental Shelf activity, preventing offsetting against other group income)
- **Supplementary Charge (SC):** historically 10%–32%; currently 10% on top of RFCT
- **Petroleum Revenue Tax (PRT):** zero-rate since 2016 (was 50%; rate retained on the books for technical reasons)
- **Energy Profits Levy (EPL) — the windfall tax:** introduced May 2022 at 25%; raised to 35% on 1 January 2023; raised to 38% on 1 November 2024[^34]

### 5.1 UK petroleum receipts (HMRC official statistics)

| Year | RFCT + SC | PRT | EPL | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2023-24 | £3.0 bn | -£0.5 bn (repayment) | £3.6 bn | **£6.1 bn**[^17] |
| 2024-25 | £2.0 bn | -£0.4 bn (repayment) | £2.9 bn | **£4.5 bn**[^17] |

EPL receipts fell 20% year-on-year in 2024-25 due to lower energy prices and reduced production.[^17] The UK has not historically been an LNG-exporter peer — UKCS gas is consumed domestically or piped to continental Europe — but the EPL is the clearest contemporary international example of a windfall instrument and is therefore the operational template most often cited by Australian reformers calling for a windfall LNG export tax.[^35]

## 6) Malaysia and Indonesia — production-sharing contracts (PSCs)

### 6.1 Malaysia — Petronas

Malaysia operates a PSC regime with Petronas as the state-owned operator at the Bintulu LNG complex (30 Mt/y nameplate capacity)[^36]. The Malaysian fiscal flow runs through Petronas dividends plus taxes plus statutory cash payments for hydrocarbon resources. The most-recent reported breakdown (2024):

- **Petronas dividend to the Government of Malaysia 2024:** RM 32.0 bn[^13]
- **Petronas taxes paid 2024:** RM 26.8 bn (includes petroleum income tax, corporate income tax, state sales tax, export duties)[^13]
- **Cash payments for hydrocarbon resources 2024:** RM 13.1 bn (to Federal and State Governments)[^13]
- **National Trust Fund contribution 2024:** RM 0.5 bn[^13]
- **Total 2024 Petronas contribution to government:** **RM 72.4 billion**[^13] (~USD 15.4 bn at RM4.7/USD)
- **Cumulative since 1974:** RM 1.5 trillion[^13]
- **2023 Petronas dividend:** RM 40 bn (higher than 2024, with profits down 32%; the company maintained a high dividend through earnings volatility)[^37]
- **Petronas group LNG sales 2024:** 35.7 Mt (up from 32.9 Mt in 2023)[^12]
- **Bintulu shipments 2024:** 398 LNG cargoes / 25.13 Mt[^12]

### 6.2 Indonesia — Bontang and Tangguh

Indonesia's LNG industry runs through PSC contracts administered by SKK Migas. The 2024 dataset is incomplete in publicly searchable form, but key data points:

- **Tangguh + Bontang combined exports:** ~15–16 Mt/y
- **2024 LNG production target:** ~250 cargoes (vs 213 in 2023); ~17% diverted to domestic use
- **2022 upstream oil and gas total state revenue:** Rp 269 trillion (~USD 17 bn)[^38]
- **2023 specific state revenue:** UNVERIFIED in this dossier — SKK Migas 2023 figure not retrieved as primary source

PSCs in Indonesia and Malaysia typically split production 50:50 to 70:30 in favour of the state after cost recovery, which produces a structurally higher take than Australia's PRRT for equivalent rent at a typical mid-cycle gas price.

## 8) Why Australia's regime collects so much less

Multiple structural features compound to depress Australian rent capture:

### 8.1 PRRT is a profit-based tax, not a royalty

Unlike Qatar (production-share), Norway (78% marginal rate plus SDFI direct equity), and Malaysia (PSC plus state-owned operator), Australia's PRRT is a profit-based tax that becomes payable only once a project has recovered its costs uplifted at a high carry-forward rate (historically LTBR + 15 percentage points for general expenditure; LTBR + 5 for exploration following the 2019 Callaghan reforms).[^40] The 15-point uplift on multi-billion-dollar offshore LNG mega-projects with multi-decade construction periods means that effective break-even — the point at which PRRT becomes payable — is pushed out 20 to 40 years past first production for many projects.

### 8.2 No royalty on Commonwealth offshore waters (except NWS)

The Commonwealth waters that produce ~70% of Australia's LNG exports[^39] are subject to PRRT in lieu of royalty. The North West Shelf is the sole legacy exception: it carries a 10% wellhead-value royalty, shared 60:40 between the Commonwealth (60%) and Western Australia (40%) — but in practice the federal share in 2023-24 was estimated at approximately A$367 million net, with WA's share approximately A$660 million.[^5][^41] All other Commonwealth offshore LNG production — Gorgon, Wheatstone, Ichthys, Pluto, Prelude — pays no royalty whatsoever.

### 8.3 Project-level ring-fencing

PRRT losses are ring-fenced to the project that incurred them. A producer that has fully written off Gorgon cannot use those deductions against Pluto, Wheatstone or Prelude. The structural asymmetry is the reverse of Norway's, where consolidation across the entire continental shelf means that losses are utilised against any income stream the company has from anywhere on the NCS.

### 8.4 Gas-transfer-pricing arbitrage

Where an upstream PRRT entity sells gas to a related-party downstream liquefaction and shipping entity (i.e., the integrated structure used by every major offshore LNG project in Australia), the transfer price determines how much profit is reported "upstream" (where PRRT applies) versus "downstream" (where it does not). The 2017 Callaghan Review and the subsequent 2023 Treasury Gas Transfer Pricing Review have repeatedly identified this as a major rent leak, and Norway's Petroleum Price Council mechanism has been repeatedly cited as the model fix — but Australia has not adopted it.[^42]

### 8.5 The 2023 deductions cap — limited effect

The Albanese government's 2023 PRRT reform introduced a deductions cap (capping deductions at 90% of assessable revenue from 2023-24), with the explicit policy aim of bringing forward PRRT receipts from offshore LNG. The reform was so modest that the gas industry itself accepted it.[^43] The ATO reports the cap brought five new PRRT-paying entities into the system in 2023-24 — but the headline PRRT payable still fell from A$1,867 m in 2022-23 to A$1,483 m in 2023-24,[^1] confirming that the reform is a marginal adjustment, not a structural fix.

### 8.6 No sovereign wealth fund

Norway, Qatar, and even smaller producers like Timor-Leste maintain dedicated petroleum sovereign wealth funds with explicit intergenerational fiscal rules. Australia has the Future Fund (general-purpose, A$240+ bn) but no dedicated petroleum-revenue savings vehicle and no rule that hydrocarbon rents must be saved for future Australians rather than spent in the current budget cycle. The opportunity cost of this institutional gap, compounded since the 1980s, is in the trillions of AUD.

## 10) Caveats and methodological notes

1. **Revenue scope.** The country revenue figures combine oil and gas where they cannot be cleanly separated (Norway, US, UK in particular). LNG-only revenue would in every case be lower than the figures shown, but the gas share for Norway, Qatar, Algeria, Malaysia, Indonesia is dominant; for the US and UK it is a minority share. This works in Australia's favour in the comparison (i.e., Australia's relative position would be even worse if cleanly LNG-only revenue were used).

2. **Exchange rate convention.** USD conversions use approximate 2024-25 prevailing rates: NOK ~10.8/USD, RM ~4.7/USD, QAR 3.64/USD (fixed peg), AUD ~1.52/USD. Where exact figures matter to a footnote-level claim I have retained the source currency.

3. **LNG vs total gas.** Australia is essentially a pure LNG exporter from its offshore gas. Norway is essentially a pure pipeline exporter (~95% pipeline, ~5% LNG by ship from Melkøya[^16]). The comparison treats them as equivalent for revenue-per-tonne purposes because the Norwegian tax regime treats them identically.

4. **PRRT vs PRRT + royalty.** Australia's federal gas-specific take in 2023-24 used here (~A$1.7 bn = ~USD 1.1 bn) is the sum of PRRT (A$1.483 bn) plus an estimated Commonwealth share of NWS royalties (~A$0.25–0.37 bn). It excludes WA state royalties on the NWS (which flow to the state, not federal), and excludes the company-tax share that the offshore producers pay on residual profits (since this is comparable to and netted against company-tax payments in every other country in the comparison).

5. **Qatar fiscal scope.** Qatar's USD 85 bn 2023 figure is "hydrocarbon export revenue" as reported by IMF Article IV consultation summaries. Qatari fiscal data is less granular than OECD-member data; this figure is the standard cited reference.

6. **Indonesia 2023-24 gap.** SKK Migas Annual Report 2023 and 2024 USD-equivalent state-revenue figures were not retrievable as primary sources within the time budget of this dossier. The 2022 figure is treated as the last verifiable point; readers should treat the Indonesia row as illustrative rather than definitively comparable.

7. **Algeria caveat.** The Sonatrach $45 bn 2024 figure is corporate export revenue, not government take; treating it as government take overstates the comparison. Algeria's actual fiscal take is lower (it is what flows from Sonatrach via dividends, taxes and royalties to the budget). Marked accordingly.
