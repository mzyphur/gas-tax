# Pocock & The Australia Institute: Evidence Dossier on Gas Tax Claims

**Compiled:** 11 May 2026  
**Scope:** Primary-source verification of Senator David Pocock's verbatim public statements on Petroleum Resource Rent Tax (PRRT) and gas-export taxation, plus the Australia Institute's 25% gas-export-tax proposal and supporting research. Cross-checked against Australian Energy Producers (AEP/formerly APPEA), IEEFA, and the parliamentary record where accessible.

---

## 1. Senator David Pocock's gas-tax claims — verbatim, with venues

### 1.1 The signature "beer excise" exchange — Senate Estimates, February 2026

The viral moment was a 57-second exchange in Senate Economics Estimates between Senator David Pocock (Independent, ACT) and Treasury Deputy Secretary Dr Shane Johnson, held in mid-February 2026. The clip was disseminated on Pocock's official Facebook page and Instagram, reaching nearly 10 million Instagram views and over 8 million video views on Facebook within weeks.[^1][^2][^3]

**Pocock's question, verbatim:**

> "Would it be accurate to say that the tax on offshore gas exports — PRRT — is still giving us less revenue than the tax on beer?"[^3][^4]

**Treasury's confirmation, verbatim (Dr Shane Johnson):**

> "Taxes on beer were expected to be $2.7 billion in 2025-26, while taxes from PRRT were expected to be $1.5 billion."[^3][^4]

**Pocock's follow-up rhetorical line, verbatim:**

> "How do we live in a country, one of the biggest gas exporters in the world, and we're getting more tax from beer than PRRT?"[^4][^5]

The exchange was the basis for Pocock's subsequent Senate motion (12 March 2026) to establish a Select Committee titled — non-ironically — **"Select Committee on Why Gas Companies Pay Less for Offshore Liquefied Natural Gas than Australians Pay in Beer Excise"**. The committee comprises 2 Labor, 2 Coalition and 1 crossbench senator, with Pocock as Chair, reporting May 2026.[^6][^7]

### 1.2 The earlier nurses / HECS / beer comparison — Dissenting Report (May 2024)

Long before the viral February 2026 moment, Pocock had been making the same comparison in the parliamentary record. The single richest source of Pocock's verbatim text on gas taxation is his **Dissenting Report to the Senate Economics Legislation Committee on Schedule 5 of the Treasury Laws Amendment (Tax Accountability and Fairness) Bill 2023**, finalised May 2024 and tabled in the Senate.[^8]

**Verbatim Pocock comparisons in the Dissenting Report:**

> "In 2022-23, PRRT revenue of $2.4 billion was less than the beer excise ($2.6 billion) and less than half the HELP/SFSS revenue ($4.9 billion)."[^8]

> "In 2020-21, Australia's nurses paid $5.4 billion, while over the same period, the gas industry paid just $1.8 billion of tax (company income tax plus PRRT). Meaning that nurses paid three times as much tax as the gas industry."[^8]

> "Four major gas producers… have not paid a single cent in PRRT on combined income of $297 billion."[^8]

> "In 2021-22 the oil and gas industry paid $926 million PRRT, around 1% of their $89 billion income."[^8]

> "Offshore LNG producers have not paid a single cent in PRRT."[^8]

> "This is frankly a scandal whose enormity is obvious in the *zero* PRRT that has been paid on LNG exports."[^9]

### 1.3 Norway / sovereign wealth fund comparison

Pocock uses Norway as a recurring counter-example. The Dissenting Report (May 2024):

> "Norway has transferred this money to a sovereign wealth fund now worth A$1.9 trillion, equal to around $350,000 for each of Norway's 5.4 million citizens."[^8]

By March 2026 the comparison had been updated: in his 2 March 2026 media release proposing the Select Committee, Pocock said:

> "Currently we are squandering what Norway has turned into a $3 trillion dollar sovereign wealth fund."[^7]

And in his 4 May 2026 release pushing back on the gas industry's $21.9 billion tax claim:

> "While countries like Norway have built a $3 trillion dollar sovereign wealth fund, Australia has a trillion dollars of national debt."[^10]

### 1.4 The "Stop the Aussie gas scam" petition framing

On his petition page (no specific date; "Stop the Aussie gas scam"):

> "They're paying less in resource tax on offshore LNG exports than Aussies pay on beer."[^11]

### 1.5 Senate speech on gas as an export problem — 4 November 2025

In an urgency motion in the Senate on 4 November 2025 (Hansard via OpenAustralia.org), Pocock said:[^12]

> "Wholesale gas prices in Australia are more than three times higher than in the USA."[^12]  
> "Seven times higher than in Qatar and Canada."[^12]  
> "Eighty-three per cent of gas extracted in Australia is used for LNG export."[^12]  
> "We export more than four times as much gas as is used domestically."[^12]

He cited the ACCC June 2025 report: "122 petajoules of uncontracted gas in 2024" and "79 per cent of that was exported", framing the situation as "not a gas supply problem … a gas export problem".[^12]

### 1.6 Pushback on the industry's $21.9 billion tax claim — 4 May 2026

In the 4 May 2026 release "GAS INDUSTRY NEEDS TO FACT CHECK ITS FIGURES", Pocock argued that the AEP/KPMG headline figure obscured a downward trend. Verbatim:

> "The gas industry makes a contribution, but nowhere near enough."[^10]

> "Getting a fair return for the export of our finite natural resources can help fund the services Australians rely on."[^10]

He cited government data showing PRRT receipts falling from $1,725m (2022-23) to $1,483m (2024-25), and Commonwealth royalties dropping from $2,079m to $933m over the same window.[^10]

### 1.7 Pocock's instruments — bills, motions, dissenting reports

Pocock has *not* introduced a standalone private senator's bill on PRRT to date. His instruments have been:

1. **The May 2024 Dissenting Report** to the Senate Economics Legislation Committee on Schedule 5 of the Treasury Laws Amendment (Tax Accountability and Fairness) Bill 2023, recommending the PRRT rate be increased and the deduction cap reduced to "80 per cent or lower of assessable receipts" rather than the government's 90%.[^8]
2. **The 2 March 2026 motion** to establish the Select Committee on Gas Taxation (passed 12 March 2026; reports May 2026).[^7][^6]
3. **The 4 November 2025 urgency motion** on redirecting uncontracted gas to the domestic market.[^12]
4. **The "Stop the Aussie gas scam" petition** (18,061 signatures as of the relevant snapshot).[^11]
5. Routine Senate Estimates questioning — the source of the viral February 2026 exchange.[^3]

### Comparisons NOT found in primary sources

I could not locate verbatim Pocock quotes specifically comparing PRRT to **tobacco excise**, **fuel excise**, or **GST on takeaway food** in any first-party (davidpocock.com.au, Hansard via OpenAustralia, or Facebook page) source. The "tobacco excise vs PRRT" framing is real and well-documented — but in primary sources it traces to **Australia Institute** writers (notably Greg Jericho and Richard Denniss), not to Pocock himself. **UNVERIFIED for Pocock authorship** — see Section 2 below for the Institute's tobacco/fuel framings.

## 2. The Australia Institute — papers, headline figures, authors

### 2.1 "A stronger PRRT cap: A fairer way to tax gas super profits" (May 2024)

**Author:** Greg Jericho (Chief Economist).  
**Publisher:** The Australia Institute.  
**Date:** May 2024.[^13]

Key headline findings, verbatim from the discussion paper:

> "Over the past five years assessable petroleum revenue has averaged $34 billion per year and PRRT revenue $1.2 billion a year, representing just 3.6% of petroleum revenue."[^13]

> "Adjusting for inflation, real PRRT revenue has fallen from $3.4 billion in 1990-91 to $2 billion in 2021-22, while real assessable petroleum revenues have grown from $9.9 billion to $49 billion a year."[^13]

> "While the PRRT has the potential to be a major revenue source for the Commonwealth Government, the current arrangements mean that it collects less revenue than tobacco or beer excises, or even that paid in HELP/SFSS debts."[^13]

> "Before the opening of the Gladstone port the gas industry would routinely pay over $25 in tax and royalties per $100 of industry revenue, now it is just above $5."[^13]

Proposed alternatives and Jericho's revenue estimates (Table 2 in the report):

| Cap | 2023-24 | 2024-25 | 2025-26 | 2026-27 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 90% (government) | $0.5b | $0.6b | $0.8b | $0.5b |
| 80% (Greens) | $1.0b | $1.1b | $1.6b | $1.2b |
| 70% | $1.5b | $1.7b | $2.5b | $2.0b |
| 60% | $2.0b | $2.2b | $3.3b | $2.7b |

(Source: Jericho, *A stronger PRRT cap*, May 2024, Table 2, p.16.)[^13]

Jericho's headline claim: "Had the 60% cap been in place since 2016-17 it would have raised some $10.8bn more than was raised by the PRRT."[^13]

### 2.5 "Government revenue from LNG exports: Australia vs Qatar" — Saunders & Campbell (May 2025)

**Authors:** Matt Saunders, Rod Campbell. **Date:** May 2025.[^17] **Published number:** P1816.

Headline finding, verbatim:

> "In 2023, Australia and Qatar exported 81 MT and 80 MT of LNG, worth around $AUD 86 billion and $AUD 83 billion respectively."[^17]

### 2.6 "Gas industry claims debunked" — Rod Campbell (May 2024)

**Author:** Rod Campbell. **Date:** May 2024.[^18]

Verbatim:

> "AEP's $17.1 billion figure consists of $12.8 billion in company tax, $1.1 billion in Petroleum Resource Rent Tax (PRRT), $2.6 billion in 'excise, royalties and fees' and $0.7 billion in 'all other taxes'. > "AEP's presentation of royalties as a tax payment is like a builder claiming the cost of bricks as a tax payment."[^18]

### 2.7 The 25% gas export tax — full specification

The 25% flat tax on the value of LNG exports is *not* an Australia Institute originating proposal — the Institute attributes it to the **Australian Council of Trade Unions (ACTU)** and frames itself as supporter, modeller and amplifier.[^19] The Greens and IEEFA have aligned with the flat-tax model.[^20]

**Methodology (per Saunders & Campbell, May 2025, and the Institute's live tracker[^21]):**

- **Tax base:** 25% applied to the value of LNG exports (the export-revenue gross — not profits, not free-on-board net of marketing, but the headline value of exports).
- **Thresholds/deductions:** None disclosed. The proposal is explicitly designed to replace, not stack on top of, the PRRT.
- **Revenue estimate, headline:** "$17 billion annually" (Australia Institute, citing testimony to the Select Committee).[^22]
- **Time-cumulative:** The Institute's live tracker estimates $63.8 billion in foregone revenue from 1 July 2022 to 31 December 2025 (3.5 years) — a flow rate of approximately $49.8m/day, $2.08m/hour, $34.6k/minute, $577/second.[^21]
- **Comparator alternative use:** "Could have funded free childcare or free university and TAFE for all Australians over the same period."[^21]
- **Assumptions on gas price and volume:** Not explicitly disclosed on the public landing pages; the $17 billion figure aligns with FY2023-24 LNG export values around $68-86 billion and the Qatar comparator at $27+ billion of foregone revenue per year. Assumed FX and gas price are presumably the DISER Resources & Energy Quarterly mid-case.
- **International comparators referenced:** Norway's 90% effective petroleum tax,[^13] Qatar's combined royalty + state-ownership structure delivering ~$56b on ~$83b LNG exports in 2023.[^17]

ACTU/AI Institute polling support (October 2025): "Two in three (67%) One Nation voters and seven in ten (70%) Greens voters agree gas export corporations should pay a flat 25% on gas exports."[^19]

## 3. The beer-excise-vs-gas-tax comparison — fact-checked

### 3.1 Beer excise revenue, recent years

- 2025-26 (Treasury forecast, as confirmed in Senate Estimates by Dr Shane Johnson, Feb 2026): **$2.7 billion**.[^3]
- 2022-23 (cited in Pocock's Dissenting Report, May 2024): **$2.6 billion**.[^8]
- The Point fact-check range (Feb 2026): **$2.5–3.0 billion annually**.[^4]

Context: From 1 August 2025 the Albanese Government paused draught beer excise indexation for two years, costing approximately $165 million over five years.[^27] This pause modestly reduces the beer excise total but does not change the rank-order vs PRRT.

### 3.2 PRRT, recent years

- 2025-26 (Treasury forecast, confirmed by Dr Shane Johnson, Feb 2026): **$1.5 billion**.[^3]
- 2024-25 (Pocock citation, 4 May 2026): **$1,483 million** (down from $1,725m in 2022-23).[^10]
- 2022-23 (Pocock Dissenting Report): **$2.4 billion** (a Ukraine-war windfall peak).[^8]
- IEEFA April 2026 submission: PRRT per gigajoule of sale gas ranged AU$0.21–0.41 between FY2018-19 and FY2023-24.[^28]

### 3.3 Is Pocock's claim literally true?

**Yes — under the narrow framing.** When the comparison is PRRT-only vs beer excise:

- In 2025-26 (forecast): Beer **$2.7b** > PRRT **$1.5b** — beer wins by $1.2b.[^3]
- In 2022-23 (peak windfall year): Beer **$2.6b** > PRRT **$2.4b** — beer still wins, just.[^8]
- In 2024-25: Beer ~$2.7b > PRRT **$1.48b** — beer wins.[^10]

**Industry counter-framing.** Senator Matt Canavan publicly contested Pocock's comparison, arguing that including Woodside's company tax payments would make gas-industry tax > beer excise.[^4] The Point's fact-check found Canavan correct only in 2022-23 (the Ukraine windfall year), with Woodside paying near-zero company tax historically.[^4]

**Three legitimate framings of the comparison:**

| Framing | 2022-23 | 2025-26 (forecast) |
|---|---|---|
| **(a) PRRT only vs beer excise** | PRRT $2.4b vs beer $2.6b — beer wins | PRRT $1.5b vs beer $2.7b — beer wins decisively |
| **(b) PRRT + onshore royalties** | ~$5.0b — gas wins moderately | ~$2.4b (Pocock 4 May figure) — beer wins |
| **(c) PRRT + royalties + company tax** | Industry-claimed $17.1b — gas wins | Industry-claimed $21.9b — gas wins |

The honest summary: Pocock's framing is **(a)**, which is true on every year except 2022-23 was a close call. Industry framing **(c)** wins on absolute revenue but is rejected by the Australia Institute as conflating royalties (resource purchase price) with taxes and as overstating via member-survey methodology (53% inflation vs ATO data, per Campbell May 2024).[^18]

Note: **Wine Equalisation Tax (WET)** is *additional* to beer excise and should be added when comparing "alcohol tax" rather than "beer excise" alone. WET raises approximately $1.0–1.2 billion annually. PRRT alone is also less than beer excise + WET combined.

## 5. Industry counter-claim: Australian Energy Producers (AEP)

### 5.1 Headline claim — "$21.9 billion in taxes and royalties in 2024-25"

AEP's press release of 27 July 2025 ("Australian oil & gas industry delivers record $22 billion in taxes and royalties to government revenues in 2024-25") states:[^29]

> "The industry is expected to pay a record $21.9 billion in taxes and royalties to federal, state and territory governments in 2024-25."[^29]

**Decomposition (AEP own data):**

| Category | Amount |
|---|---|
| Company tax | $13.484 billion |
| PRRT | $1.347 billion |
| Royalties, excise & fees | $6.620 billion |
| All other taxes | $0.492 billion |
| **Total** | **$21.943 billion** |

(Source: AEP/AEP Financial Survey 2025, cited in 27 July 2025 release.)[^29]

### 5.2 AEP "myth-buster" factsheet

The AEP "Get the Facts" factsheet on tax contribution, authorised by Samantha McCulloch (AEP CEO), asserts:[^30]

> "MYTH: Australian gas companies don't pay enough tax. FACT: The oil and gas industry is one of the biggest tax-paying sectors in Australia, contributing $21.9 billion in taxes and royalties in 2024-25 to state and federal governments — equivalent to the annual cost of the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme."[^30]

> "Commonwealth taxes paid by the oil and gas sector have increased more than tenfold from $1.5 billion in 2021-22 to $18.7 billion in 2024-25, reflecting major projects moving further into their production cycles."[^30]

> "MYTH: Australian gas is given away for free. FACT: Around 70% of gas produced in Australia is from Commonwealth waters where the Australian Government's Petroleum Resources Rent Tax (PRRT) applies in place of a royalty regime, with Treasury estimating gas companies will pay $8.3 billion in PRRT over the next five years."[^30]

> "MYTH: Other countries get more tax from oil and gas companies. FACT: Countries like Norway and Qatar have significant direct government ownership and/or investment in their oil and gas sector, which means the government take on more of the risks and share more in the returns. … Norway provides an annual cash refund up to the value of 71.8% for exploration costs."[^30]

### 5.3 Methodology — does AEP include "indirect tax"?

The KPMG report commissioned by AEP, "Economic contribution of the gas industry: Final report" (18 December 2024), explicitly excludes payroll tax and GST from the tax-contribution headline. KPMG Section 3.6 (Tax and royalties) defines the gas-industry tax contribution narrowly as "company income tax, Petroleum Resource Rent Tax (PRRT), state royalties and excise" — *not* including employee PAYG, GST, or payroll tax.[^31]

However, KPMG's broader **"economic contribution"** uses Computable General Equilibrium modelling and *does* include indirect economic impacts via supply chain and income-multiplier effects, claiming 215,000 FTE jobs supported (185,000 indirect) and $105 billion GDP contribution (vs $85 billion direct).[^31] These multiplier figures are routinely cited alongside the tax figure in AEP communications, creating an impression that "tax contribution" is buttressed by the indirect-jobs claim even though the headline tax decomposition itself is not inflated by indirect tax.

**The key Australia Institute criticisms of the AEP/KPMG framing are:**

1. **Royalties are not a tax** — they are "a purchase price for petroleum resources owned by the community" (Campbell, May 2024).[^18]
2. **Survey-based vs ATO data** — AEP's number is derived from a member survey, and overstates taxes paid by an average of 53% vs the ATO Corporate Tax Transparency dataset across 2017-18 to 2020-21.[^18]
3. **Ukraine windfall masking** — the "record $17 billion" (2023-24) and "$21.9 billion" (2024-25) figures reflect post-Ukraine LNG price spikes, not structural reform. Pre-war the industry paid on average $2.6 billion company tax per year.[^18]
4. **Norway tax-refund framing is misleading** — Norway's 71.8% exploration refund is part of an overall 78% petroleum tax rate that delivers vastly higher net revenue. AEP cites the refund without the rate.[^30] (See IEEFA submission for more on Norway-style cashflow tax options.[^28])

## 6. Party positions on gas tax

### 6.1 Labor (in government)

**2023 PRRT reform (Chalmers' compromise):** Treasurer Jim Chalmers' 2023-24 Budget introduced a 90% cap on PRRT deductions, projected to raise $2.4 billion over five years. By the 2023-24 MYEFO (December 2023) the entire $2.4 billion had been wiped out by parameter changes, even though Treasury was projecting *higher* oil prices.[^13]

Treasury had originally offered Chalmers three options (per Order of 15 June 2023 docs tabled in the Senate):

1. Treasury's **preferred option** — restructure PRRT to fix transfer-pricing (netback only). No revenue estimate provided.
2. Keep the Residual Pricing Method but split profits differently.
3. The 80-90% deduction cap.

Chalmers chose option 3, the weakest, at 90% — "the option most favoured by the gas industry."[^13] On the day of announcement, APPEA (now AEP) called for "bipartisan support" — strong signal that the change was non-material.[^13]

**Albanese's position (Feb 2026):** PM Albanese publicly ruled out a gas-tax hike in response to the Pocock viral video, per multiple media reports.[^5] Finance Minister Katy Gallagher responded in Senate Estimates by saying the government had made changes to the PRRT and was looking at other reform areas like reducing superannuation tax concessions.[^4]

### 6.2 The Greens

**Adam Bandt's "Robin Hood reforms" (2024-25)** include closing PRRT loopholes, applying a 10% royalty to offshore projects subject to PRRT, and an excessive-profits tax.[^32] Parliamentary Budget Office costing: $92.3 billion over a decade from gas-tax reforms; $514 billion over a decade from the full Big Corporations Tax package.[^32]

**Bandt verbatim (20 April 2022):** "When a nurse pays more tax than a multinational, something is seriously wrong."[^32]

**The Greens' 80% PRRT cap proposal** (2023) was independently costed by the PBO at $2.62 billion over 2023-24 to 2026-27 — roughly double the government's 90% cap.[^13]

The Greens highlight the "$282 billion backlog of PRRT tax credits" that gas companies can use to delay PRRT liability.[^32]

### 6.3 The Coalition

The Coalition supported the 2023 Chalmers 90% cap as "bipartisan" reform (per AEP's public framing that there was industry comfort on both sides).[^13] No public Coalition commitment to higher PRRT located in this research. Senator Matt Canavan (Nationals/Qld) has publicly attacked Pocock's beer-excise framing, calling it "just wrong" and arguing that Woodside's company tax should be added to PRRT for a fair comparison.[^33]

### 6.4 One Nation

Per the IEEFA submission (April 2026), One Nation supports a **volume-based gas royalty**, not a flat 25% revenue-based export tax.[^28] AI Institute polling (October 2025): 67% of One Nation voters support a 25% gas export tax — a sharp split between leadership and base.[^19]

### 6.5 The Crossbench / Independents

Pocock chairs the Select Committee on Gas Taxation (March-May 2026), with the report intended to land before the 12 May 2026 federal budget.[^7] Three of the five committee seats are non-crossbench (2 Labor + 2 Coalition); only one crossbench in addition to Pocock as chair — committee makeup limits the radical recommendations possible.[^7]

### 6.6 2026 election commitments

The Select Committee is timed to feed the May 2026 budget cycle. No specific election-platform commitment from major parties on gas tax located beyond the positions above. **UNVERIFIED** for the 2025-2026 election cycle specifics beyond what is summarised above.
