**Prepared:** 11 May 2026
**Scope:** Corporate income tax paid by major LNG operators and JV partners (ATO Corporate Tax Transparency 2013-14 through 2023-24); PRRT payable; state royalties (WA, QLD, NT); North West Shelf Royalty and crude oil excise; effective government take by project.

## 2. Why corporate income tax can be near-zero on huge LNG revenues

### 2.1 The Chevron transfer-pricing precedent

The landmark Australian transfer-pricing case is *Chevron Australia Holdings Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation* **[2017] FCAFC 62**, handed down by the Full Federal Court on 21 April 2017.[^27] The case concerned a US$2.5 billion (≈A$3.7 billion) intercompany loan from Chevron's US subsidiary to Chevron Australia Holdings (CAHPL) on which the interest rate was non-arm's-length. All three Full Court judges dismissed Chevron's appeal. The amount of tax in dispute (including penalties) was widely reported as **A$340 million**.[^27][^28] Chevron subsequently dropped its application to the High Court for special leave, leaving the FCAFC decision in place.[^29]

Key Full Court holding (per MinterEllison summary):

> "as a standalone company, severed from the financial strength of its ultimate parent and corporate group, CAHPL could not secure a loan for an amount equivalent to USD 2.5 billion at the rate obtained by its subsidiary."[^27]

The Chevron decision is the touchstone in Australian transfer-pricing for related-party debt; it triggered widespread ATO compliance activity against other LNG operators on debt-loading and marketing-hub structures (Singapore "marketing hubs" being the most notable feature of Chevron, Shell and ConocoPhillips Australian operations).

### 2.2 ExxonMobil Australia

ExxonMobil Australia paid zero corporate income tax on cumulative revenue of **$24.7 billion** between FY2013-14 and FY2015-16, a fact the company defended by reference to its historic PRRT contributions of "$12 billion in petroleum resource rent tax (PRRT) since the tax was introduced in 1990".[^30] The same Wikipedia source records that ExxonMobil paid no corporate income tax over 2013-14 to 2015-16.[^30] My extraction of the ATO datasets shows ExxonMobil paid no income tax for 9 consecutive years from 2013-14 to 2021-22 on cumulative revenue of $97.9 billion.[^6]–[^14]

The ATO has issued amended assessments to ExxonMobil and is reported to be in continuing dispute over financing structures and Gippsland Basin (Bass Strait) tax; specific settlement amounts and outcomes have not been publicly confirmed.[^31] **UNVERIFIED**: I have not located a court judgment or ATO settlement disclosure quantifying any ExxonMobil settlement amount.

### 2.3 Shell Energy

The Federal Court decision *Commissioner of Taxation v Shell Energy Holdings Australia Limited* (WAD146/2021, Decision Impact Statement published 31 January 2023) concerned the deductibility of acquired exploration rights/permits in Browse and Prelude;[^32] Shell prevailed on the principal issue (deductibility of exploration costs), with the ATO publishing a Decision Impact Statement accepting the outcome. This is a case about expenditure characterisation, not transfer pricing.

### 2.4 INPEX

INPEX's flagship Ichthys LNG project itself "has never paid corporate income tax in Australia" according to Michael West Media's coverage of INPEX disclosures, with INPEX itself stating it expects to begin paying PRRT "from 1 July 2026" — i.e. has paid no PRRT to date.[^33] The ATO transparency data confirms Ichthys LNG Pty Ltd (ABN 42 150 217 299) has reported total income of $43.2bn over 2018-19 to 2023-24 with **zero taxable income every year**.[^11]–[^16]

### 2.5 The mechanisms: a summary

The repeating patterns explaining near-zero corporate tax on multi-billion-dollar LNG revenue are:

1. **Massive accelerated depreciation on multi-tens-of-billion-dollar capex.** Gorgon (~US$54bn capex), Wheatstone (~US$34bn), Ichthys (~US$45bn) and Prelude (~US$15bn) generate decades of deductible depreciation. Treasury's 2017 PRRT Review notes that "the large stock of deductible expenditures will curtail" PRRT revenue for the foreseeable future.[^34]
2. **Debt loading and thin capitalisation**, including related-party loans at above-arm's-length interest rates (Chevron) and external debt structured to maximise interest deduction.[^27]
3. **Marketing-hub structures**, where Australian production is sold to a Singapore (Chevron, Shell) or other low-tax marketing affiliate at a transfer price that shifts margin offshore.
4. **Voluminous exploration and starting-base deductions** carried forward and uplifted at LTBR+15ppt (for exploration) and LTBR+5ppt (general expenditure) under PRRT, generating deductible balances of hundreds of billions of dollars system-wide.[^34]
5. **Tax consolidation** — group-level offsetting against losses or available capital allowances.

Treasury's 2023-24 Budget acknowledged the failure of PRRT on LNG: *"not a single LNG project has paid any Petroleum Resource Rent Tax (PRRT), and many are not expected to pay until the 2030s"*.[^26]

## 3. State Royalties from Gas

### 3.1 Western Australia

Most WA-produced LNG is from **offshore Commonwealth waters** (Gorgon, Wheatstone, Pluto, Ichthys — Browse and Scarborough when on-stream) and pays **PRRT to the Commonwealth, not WA state royalty.** The single major exception is the North West Shelf project (see Section 5 below), which is grandfathered onto a pre-PRRT Commonwealth ad valorem royalty (with ~2/3 of receipts passed to WA as grants).

The WA Treasury 2024-25 Budget Paper No. 3 reports the "Petroleum – State component" of WA royalty income at just $16m (FY22-23 actual) and $20m (FY23-24 estimated), rising to $33m (FY24-25 budget) and projected at $25m by FY27-28.[^35] This stands in dramatic contrast to iron ore royalties: $9,079m (FY22-23) and a budgeted $6,329m for FY24-25.[^35]

The Department of Energy, Mines, Industry Regulation and Safety (DEMIRS) **Mineral and Petroleum Statistics Digest 2023-24** records all royalty receipts collected by the WA Government — including the State's share of NWS grants — by commodity:[^36]

| Commodity | 2022-23 royalty receipts $m | 2023-24 royalty receipts $m |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Iron ore | 9,393.1 | 10,372.3 |
| Gold | 436.0 | 496.8 |
| Lithium | 929.9 | 710.2 |
| Nickel | 139.7 | 98.5 |
| **Petroleum (state-administered share)** | **78.7** | **101.3** |
| North West Shelf grants (Commonwealth share to WA) | 1,528.4 | 694.4 |
| **Total WA royalty receipts** | **11,227.7** | **12,006.3** |

The WA Treasury's own commentary in BP3 underscores the magnitude of the petroleum royalty decline: 2024-25 royalty income is projected to fall **$3.5bn (-30.9%)** to $7.8bn, of which "Petroleum – State component" makes up only $33m (~0.4%).[^35]

The Australia Institute notes that beyond NWS, WA petroleum royalties are negligible because Chevron's Gorgon, Wheatstone, Woodside's Pluto and Shell's Prelude **pay no WA royalty** at all — they are entirely in Commonwealth waters under the *Offshore Petroleum and Greenhouse Gas Storage Act* regime.[^37]

### 3.2 Queensland — petroleum and gas royalties (CSG-LNG, conventional, oil)

Queensland's three CSG-LNG plants on Curtis Island near Gladstone (APLNG, QCLNG/Shell, GLNG/Santos) source feed gas mostly from onshore coal-seam-gas leases on Queensland land, which **do pay state royalty**. QLD royalties are levied by the *Petroleum and Gas (Production and Safety) Act 2004*; the rate regime was overhauled in 2022.

### 3.3 Northern Territory — petroleum royalties

The NT Treasurer's Annual Financial Report 2024-25 reports total Territory **Royalty income** of $314.8m (2024-25) up from $274.8m (2023-24).[^40] This is the all-commodity figure (mining + petroleum) for the General Government Sector.

The NT's offshore-gas-attributable interests are Bayu-Undan (in winding-down, replaced by Barossa from late 2025), Blacktip (domestic gas) and the Ichthys offshore field tied to Darwin LNG.[^41] These projects pay PRRT (Commonwealth) on their offshore activities and do not generate NT state royalty. NT's onshore gas (Beetaloo) is at exploration stage with no royalty receipts of note.

### 3.4 Other states

Victoria, NSW, SA each have minor onshore gas royalty regimes; receipts are typically <$50m/yr each and not material to the national gas tax picture. **UNVERIFIED** — I have not extracted Victorian or SA state budget royalty line items for this public dossier.

### 4.2 Crude oil excise

Crude oil excise applies to (i) most pre-1990 onshore oil fields, (ii) the North West Shelf area (now mostly displaced by PRRT post-2012), (iii) Barrow Island offshore (specific exemption regime). The Excise Tariff Act 1921 still applies, with rates dependent on the year of discovery and graduated by production volume. The condensate exemption was removed in 2008-09.[^34]

The Commonwealth Final Budget Outcome 2023-24 does not split crude oil excise from "Other fuel products" ($1,999m, 2023-24 receipts).[^42] The Treasury 2017 PRRT Review notes that crude oil excise revenue averaged just 0.05% of GDP from 2000-01 to 2011-12 and has been declining since.[^34] Most of the historical crude oil excise base has been replaced by PRRT under the 1 July 2012 extension legislation, which subsumed the NWS project, onshore fields and Bass Strait into PRRT (with grossed-up credit for royalties/excise paid).[^34]

**UNVERIFIED**: I was unable to pull a single-line crude oil excise figure for 2023-24 for this public dossier. The Commonwealth FBO aggregates it within "Other fuel products" ($1,999m for 2023-24 receipts).[^42]

### 4.3 PRRT vs royalty interaction (offshore vs onshore)

- **Offshore Commonwealth waters**: PRRT only; no state royalty. (Exception: NWS — pays Commonwealth royalty under grandfathered regime; royalties grossed up and credited against PRRT post-2012.)
- **Onshore / state waters**: State royalty + Commonwealth company tax; some legacy projects also have PRRT under the 1 July 2012 extension, but onshore projects are generally not expected to pay PRRT because of their starting-base deductions.[^34]
- **Bass Strait**: PRRT (replaced the original Bass Strait royalty/excise in 1991).[^31]

## 5. North West Shelf Royalty — Special Case

NWS is the **only major LNG project in Australia paying a Commonwealth ad valorem royalty** (its 1985-vintage State Agreement regime was grandfathered when PRRT was extended in 2012). Under section 84-15 of the *Petroleum (Submerged Lands) Act 1967*, the Commonwealth collects royalties on NWS production; per a long-standing agreement, **~2/3 (specifically ~65%)** is passed to Western Australia as a general-purpose grant.[^44]

### Comparison to Norway

Norwegian Petroleum Directorate publishes an effective government take of **~78%** on petroleum profits (combined CIT 22% + Special Tax 56% = 78% marginal rate on cash flow basis).[^45] **UNVERIFIED in primary source this public dossier** — figure widely cited in Australian commentary (e.g., AI P1289 "War gains: LNG windfall profits 2022" and PRRT Review Section 2.4 which compares Norway/Qatar with Australia).[^34]

Australia's *effective* take from LNG (CIT + PRRT + royalty + excise) is **much lower** — Australia Institute notes Qatar collected $26.6 billion in royalties on LNG exports in a comparable year while Australia collected $0.8 billion in PRRT (in 2019-20), or 23% vs 2% of LNG sales value.[^34]
