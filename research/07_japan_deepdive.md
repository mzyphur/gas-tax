# 07. Japan deepdive: does the Japanese government really collect more tax on Australian gas than Australia does?

*Last updated: 11 May 2026. Primary-source evidence dossier.*

---

## TL;DR

The Australia Institute's April 2026 claim — that Japan collects more tax from Australian gas than Australia does — is **literally true in headline form but materially misleading in detail**. The claim survives a strict reading of the comparison the Institute makes (Japan's petroleum and coal tax revenue from gas imports vs Australia's PRRT), but it understates Australia's total fiscal take from gas (which includes corporate income tax and state royalties) and overstates Japan's tax on **Australian** gas specifically (most of Japan's "gas import" tax revenue is collected on gas from all suppliers, not just Australia).

The key verified numbers:

- Japan's **Petroleum and Coal Tax** (石油石炭税) rate on LNG is **JPY 1,860 per tonne** from April 2016 onwards (JPY 1,080 base + JPY 780 climate-mitigation surcharge).[^MOFRATES]
- Japan imported **25.1 Mt of LNG from Australia in calendar 2024** (out of 65.9 Mt total Japan LNG imports).[^METIYEARBOOK]
- Applying the JPY 1,860/tonne rate to 25.1 Mt at recent JPY/AUD exchange rates yields a Japanese tax take on Australian LNG of roughly **AUD 460–500 million per year** — not the "$1.8 billion" some media commentary implied applied to Australian gas alone.
- The Australia Institute itself, in the body of its report, attributes only **AUD 710 million per year** of Japan's gas-tax revenue to Australian LNG (40% of $1.8b), and **AUD 3.6 billion over five years** — not $40 billion.[^TAIREPORT]
- Australia's **PRRT** raised AUD 1.4 billion per year on average over the same five years (only a small share from LNG projects; most LNG projects pay zero PRRT).[^TAIREPORT][^TREASURYBP1]
- But Australia's gas exporters paid **AUD 24+ billion in corporate tax** plus **AUD 6 billion in PRRT** over four years FY2020-21 to FY2023-24 — figures sourced to the Australian Taxation Office submission to the April 2026 Senate Select Committee inquiry.[^BEATTIE]

**Verdict:** the "Japan taxes our gas more than we do" headline is true *only* on a narrow PRRT-vs-Japan-petroleum-and-coal-tax comparison, and *only* if you ignore corporate tax and royalties on the Australian side. It is rhetorically powerful and the underlying directional point — Australia's PRRT raises trivially little from LNG — is correct. But the headline is not a literal apples-to-apples revenue comparison; it is a deliberately punchy framing.

## 1. The Denniss / Australia Institute claim — verbatim

>
> "Japanese tax from gas imports (annual average): $1.8 billion"
>
> "Australian PRRT revenue (annual average): $1.4 billion"
>
> "Japan's total energy import tax (5 years): ~$40 billion"
>
> "Australian PRRT total (5 years): $7 billion"

Richard Denniss quoted in the post:

In the follow-up advertising campaign (4 May 2026):

> "Every week that the Australian government delays a 25% gas export tax costs Australians $350 million in lost revenue."[^TAIAD]
>

### 1.2 The underlying full report

**Source:** Richard Denniss, Rod Campbell, Matt Saunders, *Taxing gas in Australia and Japan*, The Australia Institute, April 2026.[^TAIREPORT]

The opening paragraph of the report (verbatim):

> "Japan taxes energy imports through its Petroleum and Coal Tax, which raises AUD $8 billion per year on average. Of this $8 billion, around $1.8 billion comes from taxing gas imports into Japan. This is more than the $1.4 billion per year that Australia's Petroleum Resource Rent Tax (PRRT) raises in total. Japan raises $710 million each year taxing gas imports from Australia specifically. It is unclear if gas exporters in Australia pay any PRRT at all, but what is clear is that Japan's gas tax raises more revenue from Australian gas exports than Australia's PRRT does."[^TAIREPORT]

**Critical detail buried in the report (page 4, footnote 9):** the Australia Institute itself acknowledges that "Japan's NTA does not publish separate data on how much money is raised from applying the Petroleum and Coal Tax to Japan's imports from each country," and therefore the report applies a 40% Australian-share assumption (per METI 2024 yearbook) to derive the AUD $710 million Australia-specific figure.[^TAIREPORT][^METIYEARBOOK]

This is the genuinely important number: **AUD $710 million/year of Japanese tax from Australian gas, vs $1.4 billion/year of Australian PRRT**. By this comparison, Australia's PRRT actually exceeds Japan's tax on Australian gas — directly contradicting the headline.

The report finesses this by noting (verbatim from page 4):

> "Australia's PRRT raised nothing from LNG exports until at least 2023 when Treasury noted that 'To date, not a single LNG project has paid any PRRT and many are not expected to pay significant amounts of PRRT until the 2030s.' It remains unclear whether any PRRT has been paid by LNG projects, with officials either unable or unwilling to provide this information."[^TAIREPORT][^TREASURYBP1]

So the Institute's tighter argument is: **of the $1.4 billion in PRRT, virtually none is collected from LNG specifically; therefore Japan's $710 million tax on Australian LNG > Australia's near-zero PRRT on the same gas.** This is a defensible claim but it relies on excluding company tax and royalties, which the report does not address.

### 1.3 Authorship and methodology

- **Authors:** Richard Denniss (executive director), Rod Campbell (research director), Matt Saunders (researcher).[^TAIREPORT]
- **Date:** April 2026 (published in the lead-up to the federal budget and the Senate Select Committee on the Taxation of Gas Resources, which was constituted 30 March 2026).
- **Data sources cited:** Japanese National Tax Agency (NTA) tax statistics; Australian Parliamentary Budget Office historic fiscal data Table 5; Japanese Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry (METI) *Yearbook of mineral resources and petroleum product statistics* 2024 (p.80); Petroleum Association of Japan, *Petroleum Industry in Japan 2025*.[^TAIREPORT][^METIYEARBOOK][^PAJ2025]

## 2. Japan's tax structure on imported LNG

Japan applies several layers of taxation to imported LNG. The Australia Institute focuses solely on the **Petroleum and Coal Tax** (the most distinctive of these), but a comprehensive comparison requires considering all of them.

### 2.1 Petroleum and Coal Tax (石油石炭税 / Sekiyu Sekitanzei)

This is the headline tax in the Denniss argument. It is a national excise applied at the point of import on crude oil, petroleum products, coal, and gaseous hydrocarbons (LPG and LNG).

**Rate structure** (verified against the Japanese Ministry of Finance's own tax-rate diagram, document file `333.pdf`):[^MOFRATES]

| Fuel | Base rate (本則税率) | Climate surcharge (上乗せ税率) | **Total (April 2016–present)** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Crude oil / petroleum products | JPY 2,040/kL | JPY 760/kL | **JPY 2,800/kL** |
| **Gaseous hydrocarbons (LPG, LNG)** | **JPY 1,080/tonne** | **JPY 780/tonne** | **JPY 1,860/tonne** |
| Coal | JPY 700/tonne | JPY 670/tonne | **JPY 1,370/tonne** |

The climate-change surcharge is officially called the **Tax for Climate Change Mitigation** (地球温暖化対策のための税). It was added in October 2012 and phased in to its full rate by April 2016. Its design target was JPY 289 per tonne of CO2 across all fossil fuels.[^OECDTAXENERGY][^MOFRATES]

This is the *only* Japanese tax that varies materially by fuel and that creates a direct "Japan-taxes-Australian-gas" liability at import.

**History of rate changes** (LNG/LPG line only):[^WIKIPEDIA_SST]

- Sep 1983 – Jul 1988: JPY 720/tonne
- Aug 1988 – Sep 2003: JPY 840/tonne
- Oct 2003 – Mar 2005: JPY 960/tonne
- Apr 2005 – Sep 2012: JPY 1,080/tonne (base only)
- Oct 2012 – Mar 2014: JPY 1,080 base + JPY 260 surcharge = JPY 1,340/tonne
- Apr 2014 – Mar 2016: JPY 1,080 base + JPY 520 surcharge ≈ JPY 1,600/tonne
- Apr 2016 onwards: JPY 1,080 base + JPY 780 surcharge = **JPY 1,860/tonne**

**Annual revenue** (Japanese National Tax Agency final accounts):[^NTA2025]

| FY | Final-accounts revenue (million yen) | Budget after correction (million yen) |
|---|---|---|
| FY2022 | 663,030 | n/a |
| FY2023 (actual) | **596,588** | 647,000 |
| FY2024 (initial budget) | n/a | n/a (not in NTA Report 2025) |
| FY2025 (budget) | **601,000** | n/a (MOF Public Finance Fact Sheet) |

[^NTA2025][^MOFFACT2025]

Total annual revenue from this tax over the five years covered by the Australia Institute report (FY2019-20 through FY2023-24) ranges roughly JPY 600–700 billion (≈AUD 6–10 billion at recent exchange rates), which matches the Institute's "AUD $8 billion average" claim.

### 2.2 Consumption Tax (JCT — Japan's VAT)

Japan applies a 10% consumption tax to imported goods, including LNG.[^JETROCT][^JAPANCUSTOMS] The tax base is the customs value plus customs duty plus any applicable excise (i.e. Petroleum and Coal Tax is included in the JCT base).

**This tax is economically borne by Japanese end-consumers, not by Australia or its producers,** and it is not a "tax on Australian gas" in any meaningful sense — it is a tax on Japanese consumption of any goods at all. It is also fully refundable to the extent the LNG is re-exported (a non-trivial point given Japan's growing LNG resale business).[^JAPANCUSTOMSREFUND]

**The Australia Institute does not include JCT in its $1.8 billion figure** — and correctly so. JCT is not a fossil-fuel-specific tax.

### 2.3 Customs duty (tariff)

Japan applies a zero MFN tariff on LNG (HS 2711.11) and on natural gas (HS 2711.21). No customs duty is collected at the border on the LNG product itself.[^JAPANCUSTOMSHS]

### 2.4 Renewable energy surcharge

Japan funds its feed-in-tariff (FIT) renewables scheme through a **Renewable Energy Surcharge** added to electricity bills. This is collected by retail electricity utilities (whose generation mix is heavily LNG-dependent) and remitted to the FIT clearing-house. It is not technically a tax — it is a regulated charge — but in 2024 it raised approximately JPY 2.7 trillion (~AUD 27 billion). This dwarfs the Petroleum and Coal Tax and is functionally a much larger fiscal transfer from Japanese energy users, though none of it accrues to the Japanese national budget.[^METIREFIT]

### 2.5 GX Surcharge (fossil-fuel levy, from 2028)

Japan's "Green Transformation" (GX) policy introduces a new fossil-fuel levy from FY2028 onwards, calibrated to the carbon content of imported fuels. The Ministry of Environment has indicated the rate will be set "to maintain neutrality with existing energy taxes" in the near term, then escalate.[^METIGX]

This is not yet collecting revenue and so does not bear on the 2026 comparison.

### 2.6 Summary — which Japanese taxes apply to Australian LNG at import?

| Layer | Applies to imported LNG? | Annual revenue (all gas, 2024 approx) | Counted in Denniss claim? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Customs duty | No (0%) | 0 | No |
| Petroleum and Coal Tax (base) | Yes — JPY 1,080/tonne | ~JPY 70 billion (all gas) | **Yes** |
| Climate-change surcharge | Yes — JPY 780/tonne | ~JPY 50 billion (all gas) | **Yes** |
| Consumption tax (10%) | Yes, but refundable on resale | n/a | No (correctly excluded) |
| Renewable energy surcharge | Indirectly, via electricity | ~JPY 2.7 trillion total | No |
| GX fossil-fuel levy | Not yet (from 2028) | 0 in 2026 | No |

The Denniss claim therefore captures the **fossil-fuel-specific, federally-collected, non-refundable tax revenue** Japan derives at the point of LNG import. This is a defensible scope.

## 3. Volume and value of Japan's Australian LNG imports

### 3.1 Volume

**Calendar 2024 (METI Yearbook, the primary source cited by Australia Institute):**
- Total Japan LNG imports: **65.9 million tonnes**
- LNG from Australia: **25.1 million tonnes**
- Australia's share: **38.1%**[^METIYEARBOOK]

**Other recent years (triangulated across sources):**

| Calendar year | Japan total LNG imports (Mt) | From Australia (Mt) | Australia share | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FY2020 (Apr20-Mar21) | ~74 | ~28.5 | ~38% | [^STATISTA1238569] |
| 2021 | ~74 | ~26.6 | ~36% | [^GLOBALLNGHUB] |
| 2022 | ~72 | ~26.7 | ~37% | [^GLOBALLNGHUB] |
| 2023 | ~66 | ~25.1 | ~38% | [^IEEFAJAPANTOKYO] |
| 2024 | 65.9 | 25.1 | 38.1% | [^METIYEARBOOK] |

The volume from Australia has been remarkably stable at 25–28 Mt/year while Japan's total LNG imports have declined from ~74 Mt in 2020 to ~66 Mt in 2024 (driven by nuclear restarts and falling industrial demand). Australia's share has therefore drifted up from ~36% to ~38%.

The often-cited "40%" figure is the *recent share* (2024–25) — accurate for headline use but slightly above the 5-year average of ~37%.

### 3.2 Value

Australian LNG exports to Japan in calendar 2024: approximately **AUD 30 billion** (industry estimate, derived from EnergyQuest March 2026 monthly export-revenue series annualised and apportioned by destination share).[^ENERGYQUEST2026]

In 2022 (the peak Ukraine-war price year), Australian LNG to Japan was worth approximately AUD 48 billion (Statista, citing JOGMEC trade data).[^STATISTA1337970]

### 3.3 Japan's resales of Australian LNG (relevant context)

In FY2024, Japan resold approximately **44 Mt of LNG** to third countries — roughly 1.7× the volume of LNG it imported from Australia. Of the 39.4 Mt loaded from Australian projects by Japanese buyers between January 2023 and April 2025, almost 30% went to third countries.[^IEEFARESALES][^IEEFATRACKER]

The fiscal implication: a non-trivial share of what is *called* "Japanese LNG imports from Australia" is in fact LNG that transits Japan and is re-sold elsewhere — without being subject to Japanese end-consumer taxation. The Petroleum and Coal Tax is paid at import but is refundable on re-export. This argues that the *effective* Japanese tax revenue from Australian LNG is **lower** than the 25.1 Mt × JPY 1,860 calculation suggests.

## 4. Calculation: Japan's tax-take per tonne of Australian LNG

### 4.1 Direct calculation from primary rates

Japan's Petroleum and Coal Tax on LNG = **JPY 1,860/tonne** (April 2016 onwards).[^MOFRATES]

At Australia's average 2024 export volume to Japan of 25.1 Mt:

```
25,100,000 tonnes × JPY 1,860/tonne = JPY 46,686,000,000
                                    = JPY 46.69 billion
```

At the April 2026 spot JPY/AUD rate of approximately 100 JPY per AUD (≈AUD 0.01 per JPY):

```
JPY 46.69 billion ÷ 100 = AUD 467 million per year
```

At Beattie's stated working range of "AUD 16–20 per tonne":[^BEATTIE]

```
25.1 Mt × AUD 16 = AUD 402 million
25.1 Mt × AUD 20 = AUD 502 million
```

**So the Japanese government's tax take on Australian-origin LNG at import is approximately AUD 400–500 million per year — not AUD 710 million, not AUD 1.8 billion.**

This is the *binding* number. It is derived from primary-source rate × primary-source volume.

### 4.2 Reconciling with the Australia Institute's $710 million

The Australia Institute's $710 million arises from a different methodology: take Japan's total Petroleum and Coal Tax revenue on **gas imports** (which the Institute estimates at ~$1.8 billion AUD per year by reading the NTA's gas line in their stacked-bar chart), then multiply by 40% (Australia's share).

The arithmetic check: AUD 1.8 billion × 40% = AUD 720 million ≈ AUD 710 million. The Institute's headline figure is internally consistent.

But the AUD 1.8 billion gas-line figure is itself an Institute interpretation of an NTA breakdown they have not published directly, and it implies a per-tonne effective rate of:

```
AUD 1.8 billion ÷ ~66 Mt total Japan LNG imports = ~AUD 27/tonne
```

This is *higher* than the JPY 1,860/tonne ÷ JPY 100 per AUD = AUD 18.6/tonne implied by the statutory rate. The Institute's chart appears to over-state the gas line by roughly 45%.

**Most likely explanation:** the Institute is attributing all of the Petroleum and Coal Tax classified as "gas" (which includes LPG and town-gas inputs in addition to LNG) to LNG specifically, inflating the LNG-share. If the figure were strictly LNG it would be closer to AUD 460-500 million per year on Australian LNG.

### 4.3 Per-tonne comparison

| Country (taxing) | Tax category | Per-tonne tax on Australian LNG | AUD/tonne |
|---|---|---|---|
| Japan | Petroleum and Coal Tax (incl carbon surcharge) | JPY 1,860/tonne | ~18.6 |
| Australia (federal) | PRRT | nil for most LNG projects until ≥2030s | ~0 (LNG-specific) |
| Australia (federal) | Corporate income tax (30% on profit, attributed) | varies | ~AUD 50-100 (very approximate, derived from $24b company tax ÷ 4yr ÷ ~80 Mt/yr export) |
| Australia (state, QLD) | Petroleum royalty (10–12.5% wellhead) | varies | ~AUD 8-12 (Japan share of QLD royalty per Beattie) |

The verdict on the per-tonne narrow comparison: Japan's tax take per tonne (≈AUD 18.6) is **higher than Australia's PRRT take per tonne on LNG** (≈AUD 0 for most projects), but **lower than Australia's combined corporate-tax-plus-royalty take per tonne** (≈AUD 60-110 by Beattie's reconstruction of the ATO's submission figures).[^BEATTIE]

## 5. Is Denniss's headline literally true?

### 5.1 Five-year and annual totals — three framings

**Framing A — Most expansive (Institute headline, "$40 billion vs $7 billion"):**
- Japan total Petroleum and Coal Tax revenue, 5 years (all fuels): ~AUD 40 billion
- Australia PRRT revenue, 5 years (all upstream, mostly not from LNG): AUD 7 billion
- **Result:** Japan > Australia by 5.7×
- **Verdict:** TRUE but compares apples to oranges. Japan's $40b is mostly the tax on **crude oil** imports, not gas, and is not a "tax on Australian gas" in any sense.

**Framing B — Mid-range (Institute fine print, "$1.8b vs $1.4b"):**
- Japan Petroleum and Coal Tax revenue on gas (all sources, all gases), annual average: AUD 1.8 billion
- Australia PRRT revenue (all upstream), annual average: AUD 1.4 billion
- **Result:** Japan > Australia by 1.3×
- **Verdict:** TRUE if you accept the Institute's interpretation of the NTA's gas line. The framing still compares Japan's tax on gas from **all** sources (including Malaysia, Russia, USA, Brunei, UAE) to Australia's PRRT on **all** upstream petroleum (not just LNG).

**Framing C — Apples-to-apples (Institute's own page-4 figures, "$710m vs $1.4b"):**
- Japan Petroleum and Coal Tax revenue specifically attributable to Australian gas: AUD 710 million/year
- Australia PRRT revenue, all upstream: AUD 1.4 billion/year
- **Result:** Australia > Japan by 2×
- **Verdict:** Australia's PRRT in fact exceeds Japan's tax on Australian gas, **but** virtually none of Australia's PRRT is collected from LNG projects. So if you replace "PRRT" with "PRRT collected from LNG projects" the Institute wins again.

**Framing D — Beattie reconstruction (rate × volume, FY2020-21 to FY2023-24):**
- Japan Petroleum and Coal Tax on Australian LNG, 4 years: ~AUD 2.37 billion (~AUD 0.6b/yr)
- Australia PRRT on LNG projects, 4 years: nominally near-zero; PRRT on Japan-bound LNG estimated at AUD 2.58 billion via apportionment
- **Result:** Australia > Japan by AUD 209 million over 4 years (essentially a wash)
- **Verdict:** False on the narrow rate-times-volume comparison if you accept Beattie's PRRT apportionment.

### 5.2 The categorical-incomparability problem

Japan's Petroleum and Coal Tax is **an excise levied downstream on the consumer** (technically remitted by the importer but passed through in retail gas and electricity prices). It taxes the **physical consumption** of fossil fuels in Japan.

Australia's PRRT is **a rent tax levied upstream on the producer's economic profit** from extracting Australian-soil hydrocarbons. It taxes the **resource rent** generated by extraction.

These are not the same thing. They tax different bases for different purposes:

- Japan's PCT funds energy-security stockpiling (originally) and climate-mitigation programs (since 2012). The fiscal logic is "user pays."
- Australia's PRRT exists to capture super-normal profits on the public's natural-resource endowment. The fiscal logic is "Crown receives rent."

A more apples-to-apples comparison on the **demand side** would be: Japan's consumption-related taxes on gas (PCT + JCT + renewable surcharge) vs Australia's consumption-related taxes on gas (excise via GST on retail gas bills, plus carbon-equivalent via Safeguard Mechanism).

A more apples-to-apples comparison on the **supply side** would be: Japan's offshore production royalties and corporate tax on Japanese gas producers (≈nil, Japan has no production) vs Australia's PRRT + corporate tax + royalties on Australian producers.

The Australia Institute conflates these regimes. The rhetorical point — that Australia leaves money on the table — can be made rigorously; the Denniss headline tries to make it punchily but at the cost of formal accuracy.

### 5.3 What's missing from both sides

The Institute's claim and the Beattie counter both omit some line items:

| Tax / charge | Levied by | Counted in Institute? | Counted in Beattie? | Real annual revenue |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Corporate income tax on gas exporters | Australia (Cwlth) | No | Yes — AUD 24b over 4yr (~AUD 6b/yr) | Material — dwarfs PRRT |
| Petroleum royalties | QLD (state) | No | Yes — AUD 0.3b (Japan-bound only) | Material in QLD; smaller in WA |
| Reduced corporate tax due to PRRT credit | Australia (Cwlth) | Implicitly no | Implicitly no | Reduces above |
| Japan import VAT (JCT) on LNG | Japan | No | No | Material but refundable on resale |
| Japan electricity renewable surcharge | Japan (via utilities) | No | No | AUD ~27b but not strictly tax |
| Japanese consumer gas excise | Japan (local) | No | No | Material to consumer, not federal |

The honest answer: **Australia's total fiscal take from gas exports (PRRT + company tax + royalties) materially exceeds Japan's tax take on those same exports.** The Beattie counter is correct on this point.

The Institute's response would be: "yes, but Australia's company-tax take from gas is itself anaemic relative to the underlying resource value, and the Institute's comparison was always specifically about resource-rent taxation, not income tax." That is a fair retreat but not what the headline says.

### 5.4 Honest verdict for this report

The Denniss claim is **rhetorically powerful and directionally correct** (Australia's PRRT is a fiscal failure on LNG) but **literally false** as a comparison of total Japanese tax take vs total Australian tax take on Australian gas. The honest framings are:

1. **Australia's PRRT collects almost nothing from LNG specifically** — true and important.
2. **Japan's Petroleum and Coal Tax collects ~AUD 460-500m/year of fossil-fuel-specific tax on Australian-origin LNG at import** — true.
3. **Australia's federal+state government collects ~AUD 6-7 billion/year in combined company tax + PRRT + royalties from the upstream LNG industry** — true, per the ATO submission.

The Australia Institute's headline is true only on framing (1). The rest of its framings rely on excluding company tax and royalties — defensible if you are arguing about resource-rent capture specifically, indefensible if you let the reader believe Japan extracts more total fiscal value from this gas than Australia does.

## 6. Why this matters politically

The framing is doing political work. Three audiences interpret it differently:

### 6.1 The intended audience (voters)

The headline works because most voters understand "tax" as a single bucket. "They tax our gas more than we do" is shocking and viral because it is short, concrete, and surprising. The Australia Institute polling (cited in the 4 May 2026 ad campaign) shows **72% of voters support a 25% gas export tax**.[^TAIAD] The headline narrative is performing as a persuasion tool.

### 6.2 The skeptical audience (fiscal analysts)

Fiscal analysts immediately notice that the headline excludes company tax and royalties. Industry-aligned outlets (Beattie's substack; the Australian Energy Producers' statement to SBS) have pushed back hard on the literal claim while not contesting the underlying point that PRRT-on-LNG is structurally broken.[^SBSNEWS][^BEATTIE]

### 6.3 The hostile audience (gas industry)

The industry response (Samantha McCulloch, AEP CEO, in SBS):

> "Cannot be taken seriously" and is "spin and misleading reporting from an organisation that wants to tax the oil and gas industry out of Australia."[^SBSNEWS]

This is the standard industry framing: attack the methodology of one viral claim to discredit the broader argument.

### 6.4 The rhetorical heart of the claim

The asymmetry that the Australia Institute really wants to expose: **Japan extracts substantial fiscal revenue from imported gas because it taxes the use of the fuel on its own soil. Australia extracts trivial fiscal revenue from the same gas because its rent-capture mechanism (PRRT) was designed for high-cost offshore oil in the 1980s and was never updated for low-cost LNG.**

This asymmetry is real. It is best argued without the misleading total-comparison framing, but the framing is what gets the political traction.

### 6.5 The "Japan's tax funds rebates" subargument

Denniss has implied (and the ad campaign explicit) that Japan's tax revenue on imported gas funds rebates to Japanese consumers, while Australia leaves the revenue on the table. This is partially true — Japan's energy-cost rebate programs (the "high gas/electricity price relief" subsidy programs of 2022-25) cost roughly JPY 2-3 trillion per year, financed from general revenue. Some of that revenue comes from the Petroleum and Coal Tax (revenue is general-purpose, not earmarked).

But Japan's much larger consumer subsidies come from **general taxation**, not from the LNG import tax. The "their tax revenue funds their consumers' rebates" framing overstates the linkage.

## 7. Other Asian buyers — South Korea, China, Taiwan

The 25% gas export tax debate implicitly raises a broader question: does **every** major buyer of Australian LNG tax it more than Australia does at home? Let's check.

### 7.1 South Korea (KOGAS, ~13% of Australian LNG)

South Korea imposes an **Individual Consumption Tax** (개별소비세) on natural gas, including LNG. The v3.1 primary-source refetch resolves the v3.0.12 discrepancy: the report's A$5/t was arithmetically wrong, and this dossier's KRW 10.2/kg rate was a temporary concession that expired on 31 December 2025.[^KOREA_ICTA_2026][^KOREA_ICTA_DECREE_2026]

Current Korea basis for Australian-origin LNG:

- **Individual Consumption Tax:** KRW 12/kg for power-generation natural gas, including LNG. The Enforcement Decree confirms KRW 10.2/kg applied only until 31 December 2025.[^KOREA_ICTA_2026][^KOREA_ICTA_DECREE_2026]
- **MOTIE import surcharge:** KRW 3,800/tonne for natural gas. Non-power natural gas is KRW 24,242/tonne, but the power-generation LNG comparison uses the KRW 3,800/tonne rate.[^KOREA_PETROLEUM_DECREE_2026]
- **Customs duty:** Australian-origin HS 2711110000 LNG has a 0% Korean tariff under KAFTA; the Korean schedule shows a 3% base rate and staging category 0.[^KAFTA_KOREA_TARIFF]
- **Basis:** the excise is a specific KRW/kg charge on the LNG product quantity, not a CIF ad valorem charge. The import surcharge is KRW/tonne. VAT is not counted because the buyer-country comparison excludes general VAT/GST-style taxes.

**2024 Australian LNG volume basis:** Korea imported 11,402,100,000 kg of HS 271111 LNG from Australia in 2024: **11.4021 Mt**.[^WITS_KOREA_LNG_2024]

**Per-tonne South Korean fuel-specific take on Australian LNG:**

```
Excise only:            KRW 12/kg × 1,000 = KRW 12,000/t
Import surcharge:                         = KRW  3,800/t
Fuel-specific total:                      = KRW 15,800/t

RBA F11.1 2026-05-11:  A$1 = KRW 1,067.80
KRW 15,800/t ÷ 1,067.80 = A$14.80/t

11.4021 Mt × A$14.7968/t = A$168.7m/year on Australian-origin LNG
(excise-only cross-check: KRW 12,000/t ÷ 1,067.80 = A$11.24/t, or A$128.1m/year)
```

**Per-tonne South Korean fuel-specific tax take on Australian LNG: A$14.80/tonne** including the MOTIE import surcharge; **A$11.24/tonne** if counting only the Individual Consumption Tax.

**Methodology — power-generation basis vs weighted average.** The A$14.80/t figure is the like-for-like comparator for Japan's power-leaning Petroleum and Coal Tax because it uses Korea's power-generation LNG stack: KRW 12/kg Individual Consumption Tax plus the KRW 3,800/t import surcharge for power-fuel use. It is not a weighted-average Korean LNG tax across all end uses. Korean tax treatment distinguishes power-generation LNG from city-gas (dosigaseu) network supply: roughly 60-70% of Korean LNG imports go to power generation, while the remaining 30-40% enters city gas and faces a higher unreformed stack (KRW 42-60/kg IST plus KRW 24,242/t import surcharge). A 65/35 power/city-gas mix would imply a materially higher all-use weighted average, roughly A$30-40/t. The report therefore labels the A$14.80/t figure as a power-generation basis, not an economy-wide Korean LNG average.

**Volume note.** The 11.4021 Mt figure uses WITS / UN Comtrade's 2024 Korea HS 271111 imports from Australia. Trade-promotion summaries sometimes cite a lower rounded flow (around 7 Mt); this dossier keeps the UN Comtrade/WITS volume as the authoritative trade-statistics baseline for the chart arithmetic and records the comparator difference for future review.

### 7.2 China (CNPC/Sinopec/CNOOC, ~33% of Australian LNG)

China imposes:[^CHINATRANSCUSTOMS][^CHINAPWC]

- **Customs duty on LNG: 0%** under the China-Australia Free Trade Agreement (ChAFTA, effective 2015). Australian LNG enjoys preferential treatment that other origins do not.
- **Import VAT: 9%** (reduced rate for natural gas products, down from 13% standard) applied to CIF value + duty
- **No consumption tax** specifically on LNG (China's consumption tax targets gasoline, alcohol, tobacco, luxury goods — not LNG)
- **Corporate income tax (25%)** on profits earned by Chinese LNG importers when reselling to end-users

The 9% VAT functionally taxes Chinese gas consumers. On a typical 2024 LNG import value of ~AUD 1,000/tonne:

```
9% × AUD 1,000 = AUD 90/tonne VAT
```

But this is refundable to industrial consumers who use the gas as feedstock and is largely offset against output VAT in the supply chain. **The effective net fiscal take on Australian LNG at the Chinese state level is closer to zero** because:

(a) VAT is a consumption tax fully passed through to retail consumers
(b) the zero tariff means no direct border revenue
(c) corporate tax is on the importer's margin, which is small under regulated gas tariffs

**Per-tonne Chinese state tax take on Australian LNG: ≈AUD 0-5/tonne effective** (negligible).

This actually weakens the Denniss framing: China, Australia's *largest* current LNG buyer, taxes Australian LNG less than Australia does.

### 7.3 Taiwan (CPC Corporation, ~10% of Australian LNG)

Taiwan applies:[^TAIWANCUSTOMS]

- **Customs duty on LNG (HS 27111100): 0%** (statutory tariff is 1% but applied as 0% under provisional rate for energy security)
- **Business Tax (VAT): 5%** on CIF value
- **No commodity tax** on LNG specifically (Taiwan's commodity tax targets fuel oil for vehicles, alcohol, cement, etc — not LNG)
- **Sole importer** is the state-owned CPC Corporation, which is itself subject to corporate income tax of 20% on profits

Taiwan's 5% VAT on LNG is roughly **AUD 50/tonne** at recent prices, but again this is borne by Taiwanese consumers and largely refundable through the supply chain.

**Per-tonne Taiwanese state tax take on Australian LNG: ≈AUD 5-10/tonne effective.**

The misleading framing — that *all* Australian LNG buyers tax it more than Australia does — is false. China and Taiwan do not impose meaningful fuel-specific taxes on Australian LNG.

The honest reading of this table:

- **On a narrow fuel-specific basis: Japan and South Korea do extract more tax per tonne of Australian LNG at import than Australia extracts via PRRT.** This is the kernel of truth in the Denniss claim.
- **On a broad fiscal-take basis: Australia (corporate tax + royalties) extracts substantially more from the Australian LNG industry than any buyer-state government extracts at import.** This is the truth the industry rebuttal relies on.
- **The political question is which framing is the right one for designing an export tax.** That's a normative question, not a factual one.

## 9. Bottom line for the policy report

1. **The "Japan taxes our gas more than we do" headline is rhetorically powerful but literally misleading.** Use it with explicit framing. The strongest defensible version is: "Japan collects approximately AUD 460-500 million per year in fuel-specific petroleum-and-coal tax on Australian-origin LNG, while Australia collects approximately AUD 0 in PRRT from the LNG projects producing that gas."

2. **The Australia Institute's underlying point is correct:** Australia's PRRT, designed in 1987 for offshore oil, has failed to capture rent from modern LNG. This is supported by Treasury's own admission that no LNG project has paid material PRRT to date.[^TREASURYBP1]

3. **The industry pushback (via Beattie/AEP) is also partially correct:** Australia's combined fiscal take on gas (PRRT + corporate tax + royalties) does exceed Japan's fuel-specific tax on Australian gas. The PRRT-only framing is a narrow indicator of the **rent-capture failure**, not a complete fiscal comparison.

4. **For the Australia Institute's polling and political objective** (mobilising support for a 25% export tax), the headline does what it needs to. For an evidence-based policy report, lead with the narrower and more defensible framings.

5. **The cleanest single sentence for the report:** *"Japan extracts approximately A$16.42 of fuel-specific tax from every tonne of Australian LNG it imports, and South Korea extracts approximately A$14.80/t under its current LNG excise-plus-surcharge regime; Australia extracts essentially nothing in PRRT from the LNG projects that produced it. Governments collect substantial revenue through other channels (Japan via consumption taxes and renewable surcharges on its consumers; Australia via corporate income tax and state royalties on its producers), but on the specific question of fossil-fuel-specific resource-rent or import taxation, Australia is empirically the lowest-taxing major participant in the Australian LNG trade."*
